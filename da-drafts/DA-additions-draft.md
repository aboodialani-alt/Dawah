## 1. Part VIII · Women, gender roles and feminism
### Every society draws a dress line: the dispute is who draws it, and why

**Thesis**
"You are imposing a dress code" does not separate Islam from its critics, because every state regulates public dress. The real dispute is over the standard and its grounds, and that is the question this entry moves the debate to.

**Argument**
- P1 If a critic's own society regulates what people may wear or may not wear in public, then "no one may regulate dress" is not the critic's real principle.
- P2 Liberal states regulate public dress in both directions: indecency and nudity laws require covering, and some also forbid covering (France's 2010 face-covering ban).
- P3 Where the line falls is itself contested and differs by place and by sex.
- ∴ The question is not whether a line exists but who sets it and on what grounds. Islam's grounds are stated in 24:31 and 33:59 (see Part V, "Hijab and modesty"), and the critic should state theirs.

**Evidence**
- *S.A.S. v. France* (European Court of Human Rights, Grand Chamber, 1 July 2014). France's 2010 law prohibits concealing the face in public places. The Court found no breach of Articles 8, 9 or 14, by 15 votes to 2 on Articles 8 and 9. It rejected public safety as a general justification for a blanket ban but accepted the aim of "living together". Dissenting judges and academic critics called that rationale vague. *Source: secondary summaries; check the judgment (no. 43835/11) on HUDOC before quoting.*
- *People v. Santorelli* (New York Court of Appeals, 1992) read the state's exposure statute narrowly and dismissed charges against women who went topless. *R v. Jacob* (Ontario Court of Appeal, 1996) acquitted a woman on the community-tolerance test. **Use with care:** neither established a general constitutional right, and rules still vary by place. They show the line is contested, not that it does not exist.
- The Part V entry gives the Islamic grounds: *khimar* means a head covering (24:31), and 33:59 gives the stated purpose, to be recognised and not harassed.

**Arabic**
وَلْيَضْرِبْنَ بِخُمُرِهِنَّ عَلَىٰ جُيُوبِهِنَّ 24:31 · ذَٰلِكَ أَدْنَىٰ أَن يُعْرَفْنَ فَلَا يُؤْذَيْنَ 33:59 (already in Part V; cross-reference rather than repeat).

**Objections**
- *"But hijab is forced by the state in some countries."* This entry does not defend compulsion; it only removes the objection that no state may regulate dress. Whether a state should enforce the ruling is a separate question that Muslim scholars and states answer differently. **[Decision needed: your own position on state enforcement, so the model answer matches it.]**
- *"Nudity laws are about exposure; hijab is about concealment. They are not the same."* They differ in degree and direction, which is exactly the point: someone chose that degree.
- *"That is tu quoque."* Part VI says to use consistency arguments only to expose double standards. This entry does not claim the hijab is right because critics also have rules; it asks for the ground of their rule.

**Practice**
Your opponent says: "Nobody has the right to tell a woman what to wear."
*Model answer:* Every state does. Indecency laws tell people what they must wear, and France bans concealing the face in public and the European Court upheld that. So the question is not whether anyone draws a line but on what grounds. I will give you mine, 24:31 and 33:59. What is yours?

**In one breath**
Every society regulates public dress, so "no one may impose a dress code" is no one's real principle; the real question is who draws the line and on what grounds.

## 2. Part VI · Debate conduct
### Run their rules, then ask which rule the case breaks

**Thesis**
Take the opponent's own stated criteria, apply them to a documented case, and ask which criterion it fails. This exposes whether their principle is complete without you asserting anything.

**Argument**
- P1 If someone says an act is permitted because it meets criteria X (consent, no harm to others), then any act meeting X should be permitted by their own principle.
- P2 Their own society restricts some acts that meet X.
- P3 So either they accept those acts, or their principle has an unstated further criterion.
- ∴ A further criterion means a substantive view of the good, which is what Part VIII, "Secular liberalism is a worldview, not a neutral referee", argues.

**Evidence**
Use only documented cases:
- France's ban on dwarf-tossing despite the dwarf's consent (*Commune de Morsang-sur-Orge*, 1995).
- *Stübing v. Germany* (ECtHR, 2012): ban on consensual incest between adult siblings upheld.
- Polygamy: Western states do not recognise it despite consenting adults. **[to confirm: cite two statutes before using in a Reel.]**

**Steps**
1. Ask them to state the criteria in their own words.
2. Offer a documented case where the law restricts despite those criteria being met.
3. Ask: "Which of your criteria does it fail?"
4. If they add a new criterion (dignity, public morality), point out they have moved from procedure to a view of the good.
5. Ask, do not conclude. A reductio shows a principle is incomplete, not that its conclusion is false.

**Objections**
- *"That is a slippery slope."* It is not one. Each step uses a case that already exists in law.
- *"Disgust is not an argument."* Correct, and it cuts both ways. In Haidt's "Julie and Mark" study people kept their judgment without reasons, though critics argue people were inferring hidden harm. Disgust alone proves nothing for either side.
- *"I accept all of those."* Then they hold a more radical position, which is a different debate; note it and move on.

**Do not use:** unsupported examples such as "people who identify as dogs are a common sight in Western streets". They are unsourced and invite a quick rebuttal.

**Practice**
Your opponent says: "Anything between consenting adults that harms no one is fine."
*Model answer:* Then why does France ban dwarf-tossing when the dwarf consents, and why did the European Court uphold a ban on consensual adult incest? Which of your criteria do those fail? If the answer is dignity or public morality, we agree that law rests on a view of the good, and we can compare views.

**In one breath**
Have them state their criteria, show a real law that restricts despite them, and ask which criterion it fails.

## 3. Part VI · Ethics
### The apple test: fact versus taste

**Thesis**
"This apple is red" is a claim about the world and can be checked. "Red apples taste best" is a claim about preference. Moral claims are usually made as if they were the first kind, so they need grounds.

**Argument**
- P1 A statement of taste needs no defence beyond preference, and nobody is obliged to share it.
- P2 A statement of fact is binding on everyone and can be checked.
- P3 When someone says "freedom means X and everyone must accept it", they use the language of fact.
- ∴ They owe the grounds that make it a fact and not a taste.

**Evidence**
The is-ought gap (Hume): moral conclusions do not follow from descriptions alone. See Part VI, "Ethics: what grounds right and wrong".

**Objections**
- *"Moral claims are shared agreements, not taste and not fact."* Then they are conventions, and conventions change; that is a coherent position but it is not "objective" in the sense the critic usually claims.
- *"You are doing the same."* Islam states its ground (Allah's wise nature, 91:7-8 for the fitrah) and can be questioned on it. The demand here is that both sides state theirs.

**Practice**
Your opponent says: "Everyone knows freedom means being able to do what you want if you do not harm others."
*Model answer:* Is that a fact like "the apple is red", or a view like "red apples are best"? If it is a fact, what makes it true? If it is a view, why must I accept it?

**In one breath**
Facts can be checked and tastes cannot; if a moral rule is presented as a fact, it needs the grounds a fact needs.

## 4. Part VIII · Additions to "Where do universal human rights come from?"

**New evidence**
- **Bentham on natural rights.** Jeremy Bentham, the founder of utilitarianism, wrote in *Anarchical Fallacies* that natural and imprescriptible rights are "rhetorical nonsense,—nonsense upon stilts". He held that rights are the creation of law. *(Posthumously published; editions and dates vary, so cite the edition you use.)*
- **Mill's harm principle rests on utility, not rights.** In *On Liberty* (1859) Mill wrote that the only purpose for which power can rightfully be exercised over a member of a civilised community against their will is to prevent harm to others, and he grounded liberty in utility, not in abstract rights.
- So the chain "utilitarian harm principle, therefore universal human rights" does not hold. If rights are grounded, it is in something else, which is the entry's question.

**New objection and answer**
- *"Human rights are just what the UN agreed; nobody 'decided' them."* The Universal Declaration was adopted on 10 December 1948 by 48 votes to 0 with 8 abstentions, among them Saudi Arabia, at a UN of 58 members. Its drafting committee included P. C. Chang of China and Charles Malik of Lebanon. **Do not say "the West wrote it".** Say instead: a small committee drafted a text for a 58-member UN, and agreement on a list is not agreement on what grounds it, which is the question.

**What to avoid**
Claims that rights are an exclusively "Western" invention. They are easily refuted by the drafting record above.

## 5. Part VI · Debate conduct
### "Where did your line come from?": a question, not a proof

**Thesis**
Asking where someone's moral line came from is a legitimate Socratic question. Using the answer as a proof that the line is wrong is the genetic fallacy.

**Argument**
- P1 People absorb many moral standards from school, media and institutions; the bandwagon effect describes the tendency to accept what is widely held. **[to confirm: cite a source for the bandwagon effect before using it in a Reel.]**
- P2 So "this feels obvious" is not evidence that it is true.
- P3 But the same applies to every tradition, including Islam.
- ∴ Use the question to invite examination of both sides, not to dismiss one.

**Objections**
- *"You were raised Muslim."* Say so: "Yes, that is why I check mine too. Can we both?"
- *"This is the genetic fallacy."* It is, if used as proof. Part VI already lists it; here it is used only as a question.

**For Muslim audiences only:** 45:23 ("Have you seen the one who takes his desire as his god?") frames following desire as a form of worship. Use it only with a Muslim audience, because for a non-Muslim it is a charge, not an argument. *(Verse number from memory; confirm against a Quran text.)*

**In one breath**
Ask where the line came from and be willing to answer the same question about yours.

## 6. Part I · Replacement for "The Mongols and the fall of Baghdad" (argument 5 of 10)

**Recommendation: demote this entry to "use with care".** Under the DA's own four tests for a prophecy (specific, risky, recorded before the event, unlikely by chance), it is an interpretation, not a clear fulfilled prophecy, for the reasons below.

**What the sources say** (checked against the open hadith dataset on 7 October 2026)
- **Bukhari 2928** (Abu Huraira): you will fight the Turks, with small eyes, red faces, flat noses, faces like leather-coated shields; and "a people whose shoes are made of hair".
- **Bukhari 3590** is a *different* narration: it names **Khuz and Kirman**. Do not cite 2928 and 3590 together as one description of the Mongols.
- **Abu Dawud 4306** (Abu Bakrah), graded hasan by al-Albani (also Hasan: Muhyi al-Din Abdul Hamid; isnad hasan: Zubair Ali Zai): people will settle a low-lying place **they call al-Basrah**, by a river called Dijlah (the Tigris), with a bridge; at the end of time the descendants of Qantura come, "with broad faces and small eyes", and the inhabitants split into three groups.

**Revised thesis**
Authentic reports describe a people with small eyes, red faces, flat noses and shoes of hair who would fight the Muslims (Bukhari 2928), and a city on the Tigris with a bridge that the people of Qantura would attack (Abu Dawud 4306). Some scholars read this as the Mongol conquest of Baghdad in 1258. That reading is an interpretation, not stated in the text.

**Revised argument**
- P1 Bukhari 2928 describes a physical type, names the Turks, and adds "shoes of hair".
- P2 Abu Dawud 4306 describes a city by the Tigris with a bridge, **named in the text as al-Basrah**, and attackers with broad faces and small eyes.
- P3 In 1258 Hulagu's army took Baghdad, ending Abbasid rule there.
- P4 Baghdad was founded in 762 and Basra c. 636, both after the Prophet's death.
- ∴ A people and a city matching parts of these descriptions appear in the historical record, on one reading of the texts.

**Evidence (all checked)**
Hulagu's siege and the fall of Baghdad, 29 January to 10 February 1258; Baghdad founded 762 by al-Mansur; Basra founded c. 636 (sources vary 636-639) as a military camp under Caliph Umar.

**Honest objections**
- *"The text says Basra, not Baghdad."* Correct. Identifying it with Baghdad is a scholarly interpretation, and the Mongols did not sack Basra. State this plainly.
- *"The Arabs already knew the Turks."* True; the argument is about the whole package, not one detail. But note the Mongol army was mixed: Mongol, Turkic and other troops per summaries, and Arab chroniclers such as Abu Shama called the invaders "Tatars". So "Turks" fits by extension, not by direct naming. **[to confirm: cite a historian such as Peter Jackson or David Morgan before using in a Reel.]**
- *"Bukhari 3590 names Khuz and Kirman."* Correct, which is why 3590 is not part of this entry.

**Removed from the old entry because unverified**
- The claim that the narrator Sa'id ibn Jumhan died in 136 AH before Baghdad was built in 145 AH.
- The Ibn Taymiyya quote and its reference (*al-Jawab al-Sahih* 2/171).
- "Baghdad had a Basra Gate".
Add any of these back only after checking the primary source.

**Practice**
Your opponent says: "The hadith says Basra, so this is nothing to do with Baghdad."
*Model answer:* Correct, the text says al-Basrah, and I do not claim it names Baghdad. Some scholars read it that way. What I say is narrower: the Prophet described a people with specific features and a Tigris city with a bridge, and the Mongol conquest fits parts of that picture. Judge it as an interpretation.

**In one breath**
A people with small eyes and shoes of hair, and a Tigris city with a bridge, are described; some scholars read this as the Mongols taking Baghdad in 1258, which is an interpretation and not a certainty.
