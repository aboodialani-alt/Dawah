Preservation of the Quran

4 arguments

The Quran's claim about itself is simple: "Indeed, it is We who sent down the Reminder, and indeed, We will be its guardian" (15:9). If that claim is true, we should find a text that was fixed early, transmitted by many people in parallel, and identical today to what the first generation recited. If it is false, we should find what we find with other scriptures: lost originals, competing editions, and a text that keeps changing as it is copied. This section sets out the positive case for preservation, then answers the four or five reports critics use to argue for loss or alteration.

Argument 1 of 4

The Quran was preserved through two independent channels at once

Thesis

The Quran reached us through mass oral memorisation and early written codices at the same time, each checking the other, so a corruption in one would have been caught by the other.

Argument
  1. P1A text is reliably preserved when it is fixed early and transmitted by many independent people who can check each other.
  2. P2The Quran was memorised in full by many Companions during the Prophet's lifetime, written down under his supervision, gathered into one codex within two years of his death (Abu Bakr, c. 632-634), and copied into a standard edition sent to every major city within about twenty years (Uthman, c. 650).
  3. P3Every later generation recited the whole text publicly (in prayer, especially Ramadan) in front of thousands, and its manuscripts are checked against living memorisers to this day.
  4. ∴The Quran meets the conditions of a reliably preserved text far better than any other scripture of antiquity.
Evidence

The written text was not an afterthought. The Prophet ﷺ had scribes such as Zayd ibn Thabit, Ubayy ibn Ka'b and Mu'awiya write each revelation as it came, and the Companions recited it in their prayers every day. When many reciters were killed at the Battle of Yamama, Umar urged Abu Bakr to gather the written fragments into one book. Zayd collected the Quran from written fragments and from memorisers (Bukhari 4986); later scholars such as Ibn Hajar explain that he required two witnesses for each verse.

Under Uthman (c. 650), disputes over regional pronunciations led to an official copy in the Qurayshi dialect. It was made from Abu Bakr's codex (kept with Hafsa) and sent to Kufa, Basra, Damascus and Mecca, and the private copies that differed were burned. The Companions agreed to this, including Ali, who said: "Had I been in his place I would have done the same."

The modern academic picture backs this up. Angelika Neuwirth, writing in the Cambridge Companion to the Qur'an, notes that newly found fragments support an early fixed text and that the "revisionist" theories of a late, evolving Quran have failed. Seyfeddin Kara (The Integrity of the Qur'an) concludes the codex can be dated with certainty to before Umar's death (23/644).

Source Seyfeddin Kara, The Integrity of the Qur'an: the codification dates to the earliest period

The radiocarbon-dated Birmingham folios (568-645 CE), the Sana'a lower text and the Hijazi codices such as BnF Arabe 328 show a text that is, in the portions preserved, very close to today's Quran. The Sana'a lower text does contain variants, which scholars debate. Corpus Coranicum lets anyone compare any verse across these early manuscripts. Dated Arabic inscriptions from the same years show the script in use: a tombstone in Cyprus is dated Ramadan 29 AH.

Objections

"Oral transmission is unreliable."

For ordinary stories, yes. But the Quran was a fixed text recited word for word, with set rhythm and rhyme, and corrected publicly in congregational prayer. A memoriser who changes a word is corrected by the people behind him. That is the opposite of a game of whispers. And the written channel ran alongside it from the start.

"Uthman burned copies, so he must have destroyed the real Quran."

What he destroyed were private copies with dialectal spellings, personal glosses or a different surah order. The text itself was checked against Abu Bakr's codex and against the memorisers still alive, who included the senior reciters. No Companion is reported to have accused Uthman of adding or removing a verse, although Ibn Mas'ud objected to the burning of his own codex and to the choice of Zayd. If a verse had been removed, his enemies would have said so.

"There are variant readings (qira'at), so there are different Qurans."

See the next argument. The readings were taught by the Prophet himself and are part of the revelation, not later copying errors.

Uthman copied from Abu Bakr's codex, compiled within two years of the Prophet's death from written pieces confirmed by memorisers, and the senior reciters were alive to check it. In one breath

The Quran was memorised by thousands, written under the Prophet's supervision, gathered within two years of his death and standardised within twenty. Today's text matches the first-century manuscripts. Few ancient scriptures combine a continuous oral recitation tradition with manuscripts radiocarbon-dated to within decades of the founder.

Argument 2 of 4

Non-Muslim scholars concede the Quran's integrity

Thesis

Even scholars hostile or indifferent to Islam admit that the Quran we have is the one Muhammad ﷺ taught.

Argument
  1. P1A concession from a hostile or neutral witness carries more weight than praise from an insider.
  2. P2A long line of Western orientalists, many of them critical of Islam, state that the Quran is unaltered.
  3. ∴The preservation of the Quran is accepted across the scholarly spectrum; it is not just a Muslim claim.
Evidence
  • William Muir, a missionary-minded critic of the Prophet: "There is probably in the world no other book which has remained twelve centuries with so pure a text."
  • Stanley Lane-Poole: there is no doubt of the Quran's authenticity, and it has come down unchanged.
  • Kenneth Cragg (Anglican bishop): there is no room for doubt that what we have is essentially what Muhammad taught.
  • John Burton: the Quran we have is the text "as it was arranged and approved by the Prophet".
  • Neal Robinson, H.A.R. Gibb, R.V.C. Bodley, C.C. Torrey, Denise Masson and Laura Veccia Vaglieri make similar statements .

Source William Muir on the purity of the Quranic text

Objections

"These are old orientalists; modern scholars are more sceptical."

The trend is the opposite. The revisionist school of the 1970s (Wansbrough, Crone and Cook) argued for a late Quran, but the manuscript finds since then (Sana'a, Birmingham, Tübingen) have pushed the text back to the Prophet's generation. Neuwirth and Sinai, who are mainstream academics, accept that most of the Quran goes back to Muhammad's lifetime, while still describing a text that developed within his community.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Modern scholars like Wansbrough and Crone said the Quran was compiled centuries later.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Their theories predate the manuscript evidence. The Birmingham folios are radiocarbon-dated to 568-645 CE, and Sana'a and Paris codices show the same text in the first century. Even critical scholars like Sinai now say the late-Quran thesis has failed.

In one breath

From Muir to Neuwirth, scholars who have no stake in defending Islam increasingly accept that the Quran text goes back to Muhammad's generation. The manuscript discoveries of the last forty years have only made that consensus stronger.

Argument 3 of 4

The qira'at are revealed readings, not copying errors

Thesis

The canonical readings of the Quran go back to the Prophet ﷺ himself, differ in only a small fraction of words, and complement each other in meaning.

Argument
  1. P1A copying error is accidental, unsupported by a chain to the source, and usually makes worse sense.
  2. P2Each canonical reading needs a sound chain to the Prophet (many scholars require mass transmission), must fit the Uthmanic consonantal outline, and must be correct Arabic. The Prophet explicitly approved different readings.
  3. ∴The readings are deliberate, authorised variations within the revealed text, not corruption.
Evidence

In Bukhari 2419, Umar heard Hisham ibn Hakim recite Surah al-Furqan differently from how he himself had learned it. He brought Hisham to the Prophet ﷺ, who had both recite and said of each: "It was revealed like this." He added that the Quran was revealed in seven ahruf (modes), "so recite whichever is easy for you".

Ibn Mujahid's survey found that only about 700 of the Quran's roughly 77,000 words differ between the readings, and almost all of those differences are in vowels or verb forms. They usually add meaning rather than contradict it:

  • Maliki/Maaliki yawm al-din (1:4): "King of" and "Master of" the Day of Judgement. Both are true of Allah.
  • Nunshiruha/nunshizuha (2:259): "We raise them" and "We assemble them". Both describe the bones of the man raised to life.

Ibn Taymiyya compares each reading to a separate verse (Majmu' al-Fatawa 13/391).

Objections

"If the text was perfectly preserved, why are there differences at all?"

Because the differences were revealed. Preservation means the text is exactly as it was given, and it was given with these readings. The Bible's variants are the opposite case: they come from scribes changing the text after the fact, as Metzger and Ehrman document (see Corruption of the Bible).

They are two authorised readings. The Prophet approved different recitations (the seven ahruf), and the canonical qira'at are held by scholars to preserve authorised recitations; how exactly the ahruf relate to the qira'at is discussed among scholars. They share the same consonantal text, differing in about 1% of words, mostly vowels, with complementary meanings like 'King' and 'Master' of the Day of Judgement. Preservation means preserved as revealed, and it was revealed with these readings.

In one breath

The readings are authorised by the Prophet, transmitted by mass chains, limited to about 1% of words, and they enrich the meaning. Calling them "errors" misunderstands what they are.

Argument 4 of 4

The "missing verses" reports answered

Thesis

The reports critics cite (the goat, Ubayy's two extra surahs, Ibn Mas'ud's codex, the "valley of gold" and the "200 verses of al-Ahzab") are either weak, or describe abrogation that the Quran itself announces (2:106), or concern supplications rather than surahs.

Argument
  1. P1A claim of loss needs authentic evidence that a verse the Prophet ﷺ wanted kept in the recited Quran disappeared.
  2. P2Each report either has a weak chain, describes a verse whose recitation the Prophet himself withdrew (naskh al-tilawa), or describes text the Companions themselves did not count as Quran.
  3. ∴None of these reports shows that anything Allah intended for the final Quran was lost.
Evidence
  • The goat that ate the page (Ibn Majah 1944). The "goat" detail comes only through Muhammad ibn Ishaq, a mudallis whose solitary additions are weak (graded weak by al-Arna'ut). The verses mentioned, stoning and the suckling verse, had their recitation abrogated during the Prophet's lifetime while some rulings remained. That is why Imam Malik said the suckling text "is not acted upon".
    • Ibn Hazm: the Companions memorised the Quran, so "a sheep eating a paper changes nothing".
    • Al-Baqillani: it is absurd to think the Companions relied on one sheet under a bed.
  • Ubayy's "two extra surahs" (al-Khal' and al-Hafd). These are the qunut supplication, which Ubayy wrote in his personal codex as a du'a (al-Bayhaqi, al-Sunan al-Kubra 3142). The reports about them have chain gaps of two or three generations. Ubayy himself dictated to Zayd during Uthman's compilation (Kanz al-'Ummal 4789), so he agreed with the final text.
  • Ibn Mas'ud's codex "of 111 surahs". No physical codex of his exists. Ibn al-Nadim saw several so-called Ibn Mas'ud codices and none of them agreed with each other. Ibn Mas'ud is reported not to have written the two Mu'awwidhatayn in his personal codex (Musnad Ahmad; see Bukhari 4977), and scholars explain this as his view that they were protective prayers or that they needed no writing. Three of the canonical readings ('Asim, Hamza, al-Kisa'i) run through his students, and these include al-Fatiha and the Mu'awwidhatayn. At most, he did not write the short protective surahs in his personal notebook because everyone knew them.
  • “If the son of Adam had a valley of gold…” Ibn Abbas said he did not know whether it was from the Quran. Ubayy said they thought it was until Surah al-Takathur was revealed (Bukhari 6437, 6438, 6440), so it is a hadith or an abrogated wording.
  • "Al-Ahzab was as long as al-Baqara." The reports in Musnad Ahmad run through Yazid ibn Abi Ziyad and 'Asim, whose memory was criticised, so they are weak.

مَا نَنسَخْ مِنْ آيَةٍ أَوْ نُنسِهَا نَأْتِ بِخَيْرٍ مِّنْهَا أَوْ مِثْلِهَا

2:106
Objections

"Abrogation is just a way of explaining away lost verses."

Abrogation is announced in the Quran itself: "Whatever verse We abrogate or cause to be forgotten, We bring one better or similar" (2:106; also 87:6-7). A verse withdrawn by the Prophet ﷺ in his lifetime was not lost; it was removed by the same authority that revealed it. Loss would mean that something meant to stay disappeared, and no authentic report says that.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Aisha said a goat ate the verses of stoning, so parts of the Quran are lost.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

The goat detail comes only through Ibn Ishaq, whose solitary additions are weak. The verse's recitation was abrogated in the Prophet's lifetime, as 2:106 allows. And the Companions had memorised the Quran; Ibn Hazm said a sheep eating a page changes nothing.

In one breath

Each "missing verse" report is either weakly transmitted, describes abrogation the Quran itself announces, or concerns a supplication. None shows that anything meant to remain in the Quran was lost.

Preservation of the Hadith

1 argument

Critics, and Quranists from the other side, argue that the hadith were invented centuries after the Prophet ﷺ, so Islam's second source can be set aside. Western scholarship once leaned this way (Goldziher, Schacht). But the evidence from the first century AH, and the change of mind of scholars who studied it most closely, point the other way.

Argument 1 of 1

Hadith were written down in the first century, not invented in the third

Thesis

Written hadith collections existed in the Companions' and Successors' generation, and their contents match the later canonical books almost word for word.

Argument
  1. P1If hadith were invented in the 2nd-3rd centuries, first-century collections should either not exist or differ greatly from Bukhari and Muslim.
  2. P2First-century collections such as the Sahifa of Hammam ibn Munabbih exist, and their texts are almost identical to the same hadith in Ahmad, Bukhari and Muslim.
  3. ∴The canonical collections faithfully preserve earlier material; the hadith were not invented later.
Evidence

The Sahifa of Hammam ibn Munabbih. Hammam was a student of Abu Hurayra (d. 58 AH) and wrote down 138 hadith from him in the mid-first century. The full text survives in manuscript (the Damascus MS) and is quoted whole in Musnad Ahmad. Ninety-eight of its hadith are in Bukhari or Muslim. R. Marston Speight compared the versions (Der Islam 77, 2000). He found "almost complete identity" and "practically no sign of careless or deceptive practices".

Source Arabic Literature to the End of the Umayyad Period on the Sahifa of Hammam

Source Study conclusion: the Sahifa's material is uniformly prophetic hadith, preserved with its chains

Writing was permitted early. "Do not write from me anything except the Quran" (Muslim 3004) was an early precaution so that the two would not be mixed. The Prophet ﷺ later permitted writing:

  • "Write for Abu Shah" (Bukhari 2434).
  • He told 'Abdullah ibn 'Amr to write, "for only truth comes out of it" (Abu Dawud 3646).
  • Ali kept a written sahifa (Muslim 1370).
  • Letters were sent out with written rulings, such as 'Amr ibn Hazm's letter and Abu Bakr's book of zakat.

وَمَا آتَاكُمُ الرَّسُولُ فَخُذُوهُ وَمَا نَهَاكُمْ عَنْهُ فَانتَهُوا

59:7
Objections

"Goldziher and Schacht proved hadith were forged to support later legal positions."

Their method assumed a hadith was fake unless proven otherwise and looked at late legal texts. Harald Motzki studied the earlier Musannaf of 'Abd al-Razzaq (d. 211 AH) systematically. He concluded that its reports from figures like 'Ata' and Ibn Abbas were genuinely transmitted. He wrote that rejecting hadith as a source for the first century, a view "to which I myself adhered", deprives history of an important source.

Source Motzki: the prophetic hadith in the Musannaf are not later fabrications

Source Motzki's conclusion after studying the Musannaf

"The isnad system is arbitrary; critics just graded narrators as they liked."

Scott C. Lucas compared the grades given by three early critics (Ibn Sa'd, Ibn Ma'in and Ibn Hanbal), who worked independently. They agree on 80-87% of the narrators they all graded. Arbitrary grading would not produce that level of agreement; a consistent method does.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Hadith were collected 200-250 years after Muhammad, so they're unreliable hearsay.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Bukhari compiled from earlier written collections. The Sahifa of Hammam ibn Munabbih, written by a student of Abu Hurayra, survives and matches Musnad Ahmad almost word for word, and Motzki dated material in 'Abd al-Razzaq's Musannaf to the first century.

In one breath

First-century written collections exist and match the canonical books; the scholar who studied the earliest material most closely abandoned the forgery thesis; and the early critics graded narrators with measurable consistency. The hadith are a preserved source.

Historical accuracies & early attestation

10 arguments

The Prophet ﷺ was unlettered (7:157), lived in a desert town with no library, and the Bible had not been translated into Arabic in his lifetime. Yet the Quran gets details of Egyptian and Mesopotamian history right in places where the Bible gets them wrong, and some of those details could only be checked after hieroglyphs and cuneiform were deciphered in the 19th century. This section builds that inference step by step, then adds the external evidence that Muhammad ﷺ was a real seventh-century prophet preaching exactly what the Quran says.

Argument 1 of 10

The argument from historical precision

Thesis

An unlettered man in 7th-century Arabia stated facts about ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia that were unknown in his time, absent from the Bible and Jewish lore, and only confirmed by modern archaeology. The best explanation is a source beyond him.

Argument
  1. P1If someone with no access to a body of knowledge accurately reports details from it, details unknown to his contemporaries and absent from every source available to him, the best explanation is that he received them from a source with that knowledge.
  2. P2The Quran contains such details: the title "king" for Joseph's ruler but "Pharaoh" for Moses' ruler, Pharaoh's baked-brick tower, the Egyptian motif of heaven and earth weeping for the dead, a deified Pharaoh who still had gods of his own, and Abraham's rejection of the Mesopotamian astral triad.
  3. P3The Prophet ﷺ could not read, never went to Egypt, had no Arabic Bible, and the Bible and midrash either lack these details or get them wrong. Hieroglyphs were unreadable until Champollion (1822) and cuneiform until the mid-1800s.
  4. ∴The best explanation is that the Quran comes from the One who knows the unseen.
Evidence

Egyptian sources became readable only recently. The Rosetta Stone was found in 1799 and Champollion deciphered it in 1822. Before 1822, nobody could correctly read Egyptian royal titles or funerary texts, although medieval Arabic authors such as Ibn Wahshiyya attempted to decipher hieroglyphs. Cuneiform was cracked by Rawlinson and others in the 1850s. Several details below have been argued to fit evidence uncovered after those decipherments.

Objections

"He could have heard these from Jews or Christians in Arabia."

The test is simple: the details are not in the Jewish or Christian sources. On several of them (Pharaoh's title, the size of the Exodus), the Bible differs from the Quran. You cannot copy a correction from the text you are correcting.

"These are lucky guesses."

One lucky guess is possible. A pattern across many independent details, each matching evidence found only centuries later, is not. And in each case the Quran departs from the obvious source, which is exactly where a guesser would follow it.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Muhammad just picked up Bible stories from travelling Christians and Jews.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Then he'd reproduce the Bible's details, including its errors. Instead the Quran uses 'king' for Joseph's ruler, describes baked brick in Moses' Egypt, and names the Mesopotamian astral triad: details absent from the Bible and confirmed only by modern archaeology.

In one breath

The Quran repeatedly corrects the Bible on ancient Near Eastern details that nobody could check until the 19th century. An unlettered man copying from the Bible would reproduce its errors, not fix them.

Argument 2 of 10

"King" in Joseph's time, "Pharaoh" in Moses' time

Thesis

The Quran calls Joseph's ruler al-malik (the king) and Moses' ruler Fir'awn (Pharaoh). This fits the Egyptian use of "pharaoh" as a royal title from the New Kingdom, while the Bible uses "Pharaoh" for Joseph's ruler. Many Egyptologists call the biblical usage retrospective.

Argument
  1. P1The word "pharaoh" (per-aa, "great house") was used for the king himself only from the New Kingdom (c. 1550 BCE), especially from Thutmose III onwards.
  2. P2Joseph lived well before that, plausibly under the Hyksos, while Moses lived in the New Kingdom.
  3. P3The Bible uses "Pharaoh" for the rulers of Abraham's and Joseph's eras (Gen 12, Gen 41). The Quran uses "king" for Joseph's ruler (12:43, 50, 54) and "Pharaoh" only for Moses'.
  4. ∴The Quran is precise where the Bible is anachronistic.
Evidence

Source British Museum Dictionary of Ancient Egypt (Shaw & Nicholson): before the New Kingdom the use of "pharaoh" is strictly anachronistic

Alan Gardiner (Egyptian Grammar, p.75) and W.V. Davies (Egyptian Hieroglyphs, p.45) give the same dating. Maurice Bucaille notes that nobody at the time of revelation could have known this distinction.

Fulfilled prophecies

10 arguments

Prophecy is one of the strongest tests of a prophet's claim, because it is risky. A false prophet who makes specific predictions about the future will be exposed. The Quran and the authentic hadith contain predictions that were specific, recorded before they happened, and fulfilled. Below are the criteria, then the strongest cases.

Argument 1 of 10

What makes a prophecy count

Thesis

A prophecy is evidence only if it is specific, risky, recorded before the event, and unlikely to come true by chance. Some of the prophecies below meet these four tests; others are weaker and should be used with care.

Argument
  1. P1A prediction that is vague, made after the fact, or likely anyway proves nothing.
  2. P2A prediction that is precise, against expectations, demonstrably recorded before the event, and then fulfilled cannot be explained by luck or editing.
  3. P3When a person makes many such predictions and also has a record of honesty and no worldly motive, the best explanation is that he had knowledge from God, as he claimed.
  4. ∴The test is cumulative: each fulfilled prophecy raises the probability that the Prophet ﷺ spoke from revelation.

At the Battle of the Trench (5 AH), Medina was under siege by a coalition of about 10,000. The Muslims were starving and the hypocrites were mocking them. The Prophet ﷺ struck a boulder and announced, in this order:

  1. The keys of Syria (its red palaces).
  2. The keys of Persia (the white palace of al-Mada'in).
  3. The keys of Yemen. Within a generation all three came under Muslim rule: Yemen in the Prophet's lifetime, then Syria (636) and Persia (637) (Musnad Ahmad). That is a prophecy made at the worst possible moment, against every expectation.

Argument 2 of 10

The Romans will be victorious (30:2-5)

Thesis

The Quran predicted, at the low point of the Byzantine Empire, that Rome would defeat Persia within a few years. It did, against all expectation.

Argument
  1. P1Around 614-615 CE, Persia had taken Damascus and Jerusalem (carrying off the "True Cross") and soon Egypt. Byzantium seemed about to collapse.
  2. P2At exactly this time, in Mecca, the Quran announced: "The Romans have been defeated in the nearest land, but after their defeat they will overcome, within a few years (bid': three to nine)" (30:2-4).
  3. The Quran also said "on that day the believers will rejoice" (30:4), and the Muslims' own victory at Badr (624) fell in the same period.
  4. ∴A specific, unlikely prediction with a time limit was fulfilled.

That shows how precise the prediction is: the only escape is to deny it was made in time. But the surah is Meccan, the event was famous, and Quraysh would have mocked a failed prophecy. The early sources record a wager on it between Abu Bakr and Ubayy ibn Khalaf (Tirmidhi 3194).

Miracles, sincerity & the wisdom of the law

6 arguments

This section collects the "signs" arguments: the Quran's literary challenge, the Prophet's ﷺ refusal to exploit a natural event that could have helped him, and the practical wisdom of laws such as the ban on alcohol and the obligation of zakat. Argument 1 of 6

The Quran's literary challenge has never been met

Thesis

The Quran challenged the most eloquent Arabs of its day to produce even one surah like it. They had every motive to do so and failed, and that failure is best explained by a source beyond Muhammad ﷺ.

Argument
  1. P1If a person issues a public challenge that his enemies have every motive and ability to meet, and they fail, the best explanation is that the challenge really could not be met by human beings.
  2. P2The Quran repeatedly challenged its opponents to produce a book like it, then ten surahs, then one surah (17:88, 11:13, 2:23, 10:38).
  3. P3The Quraysh were masters of the Arabic language, were desperate to discredit him, and chose war, boycott and assassination attempts rather than the far cheaper option of producing a surah.
  4. P4Their own leading experts conceded that the Quran was not poetry, soothsaying (kahana) or ordinary speech.
  5. ∴The Quran's inimitability is real, and the best explanation is that it is not the speech of a man.
Evidence

The challenge is narrowed step by step. First the whole Quran (17:88: "if mankind and jinn gathered to produce the like of this Quran, they could not produce the like of it, even if they helped one another"), then ten surahs (11:13), then a single surah (2:23, 10:38). The shortest surah, al-Kawthar, is three lines. The bar was set as low as it could go, and it was set in public, in the market culture of Ukaz where poets competed for prizes.

The opponents' behaviour is the evidence. The Quraysh tried bribery (offering him wealth and kingship), a three-year boycott in the valley of Abu Talib, torture of the weak Muslims, and finally a plot to kill him. A short text in Arabic would have cost them nothing. They did not produce one, and those who later tried, such as Musaylima, produced lines that became a joke in Arabic literature.

The best-known account is that of al-Walid ibn al-Mughira, reported in al-Hakim and al-Bayhaqi (the chain is disputed). When the Quraysh asked him what to call the Quran so that pilgrims would avoid it, he rejected each label in turn: it is not poetry, for he knew all its metres; it is not the mumbling of a soothsayer; it is not the speech of a madman. He finally settled on "magic" only because it separates a man from his family, and Surah al-Muddaththir (74:18-25) records exactly this deliberation: "he thought and deliberated… then he said: this is nothing but magic handed down". The verse was recited publicly while al-Walid was alive and he did not deny it.

Its effect on Arabic itself confirms its status. Arabic grammar (Sibawayh), rhetoric (balagha, al-Jurjani's Dala'il al-I'jaz) and lexicography were built around the Quran as the highest standard of the language. Western scholarship notes the same: the Legacy of Islam (p. 321) describes the Quran as the standard for Arabic grammar and literary theory.

Argument 2 of 6

The Prophet ﷺ refused to exploit the eclipse: an argument from sincerity

Thesis

Argument
  1. P1A false prophet seeks to strengthen his authority; a true prophet tells the truth even when a falsehood would help him.
  2. P2When the sun was eclipsed on the day his son died, the people attributed it to his grief, a ready-made miracle. The Prophet ﷺ immediately corrected them: "The sun and the moon do not eclipse for the death or life of anyone; they are two signs among the signs of Allah" (Bukhari 1043).

Evidence

The event is historically anchored. Ibrahim, the Prophet's son by Mariya, died in 10 AH, and the eclipse of 27 January 632 CE is attested in the astronomical records; some Muslim sources place Ibrahim's death on that day, though the date of his death is disputed.

The point is psychological and historical. At this moment the Prophet ﷺ was grieving and the people were offering him, unprompted, a cosmic endorsement. A man inventing a religion for power would have stayed silent or accepted it. He instead gathered the people, led the eclipse prayer, and gave a sermon to correct the superstition.

This fits a wider pattern that even non-Muslim historians have noted. W. Argument 3 of 6

The modern data:

Argument 5 of 6

Thesis

Some hadith make claims about the body or medicine that are sometimes presented as scientific miracles. They are best used defensively, to show they are not absurd, rather than as primary proofs.

These arguments depend on how terms are defined and on small or preliminary studies. Don't lead with them. Use them when an opponent claims a hadith is ridiculous.

Argument
  1. P1A critic who calls a hadith "obviously false" must show it contradicts established knowledge.
  2. P2The hadith of 360 joints, the fly hadith and the camel milk hadith each have plausible readings that are consistent with, or partly supported by, modern findings.
  3. ∴These hadith cannot be used as proof that the Prophet ﷺ spoke falsely.

  • 360 joints (Muslim 1007). "Every human being is created with 360 joints", given as the reason each person owes a daily charity for every joint. Counts depend on what is called a joint; anatomy references give figures around 360 for synovial and other articulations, while broader counts that include all fibrous junctions reach higher (Vizniak gives about 472). The point is that 360 is a respectable count, not a guess that anatomy contradicts. Chinese and other ancient sources that mention 360 or 365 do so for calendar or cosmological reasons, and were not accessible to the Prophet ﷺ.
  • The fly hadith (Bukhari 3320). "If a fly falls into your drink, dip it fully, then remove it, for in one wing is a disease and in the other a cure." Classical scholars like al-Shashi read it as practical advice (irshad), not a command. Laboratory studies have reported antimicrobial activity in house-fly extracts and peptides (for example a 2023 review of Musca domestica antimicrobial peptides, PubMed 36619234), and some small studies report that dipping reduced bacterial counts. This is enough to show the hadith is not absurd; it is not enough to build a "miracle" on.
  • Camel milk and urine (Bukhari 233). The Prophet ﷺ prescribed these to a group of desert Arabs with a specific illness. Camel milk has documented bioactive properties. On urine, be cautious: modern health authorities warn against it because of MERS, and the hadith is about a specific remedy for specific people, not a general instruction. Yasir Qadhi and others classify much "prophetic medicine" as the Prophet's ﷺ human experience of his environment rather than revelation, citing the date-pollination hadith ("you know best the affairs of your world", Muslim 2363).
  • Ramadan fasting. Studies report benefits such as improved gut microbiome diversity, lower blood pressure, and reduced oxidative stress during Ramadan. These are good illustrations of wisdom in the law, but they are not proofs of revelation.
Objections

"Then why mention them at all?"

Because critics use them as ridicule ("Islam says dip flies in your drink"). Your goal is to neutralise the ridicule, not to win a proof.

"The Prophet's medical advice could be wrong under your own view."

Yes. Muslim scholarship distinguishes his role as a messenger from his ordinary human knowledge of agriculture or local medicine (Muslim 2363). Whether a given medical hadith is revelation is itself a scholarly question.

The 360-joint, fly and camel-milk hadith are defensible but not decisive. Use them to show critics have no real case, and build your positive case on stronger ground.

The Quran correcting earlier scripture

5 arguments

Critics often say the Quran "borrowed" from the Bible. The parallels are real, but the way the Quran handles them points the other way. Again and again it takes a biblical story or saying and corrects it: it removes human weaknesses from God, restores the honour of prophets, and fixes historical details. The Quran describes itself as muhaymin, a guardian standing over earlier scripture (5:48), and that is exactly the role we see it playing. A copyist reproduces his source. An editor with authority corrects it.

Argument 1 of 5

The Quran removes human weaknesses the Bible attributes to God

Thesis

Where the Bible describes God as tired, regretful or defeated, the Quran explicitly denies it, often in words that look like a direct answer to the biblical text.

Argument
  1. P1A true revelation from the perfect God will not attribute weakness, ignorance or defeat to Him.
  2. P2Several biblical passages do attribute these to God, while other biblical passages contradict them, so the Bible is internally inconsistent on these points.
  3. P3The Quran consistently takes the "perfect God" side and in several places answers the problematic text directly.
  4. ∴The Quran behaves like a corrective revelation, not like a borrower.
Evidence
  • God "rested and was refreshed". Exodus 31:17 says that on the seventh day God "rested and was refreshed" (Hebrew wayyinnafash, literally "caught his breath"). The Quran says: "We created the heavens and the earth and what is between them in six days, and no weariness touched Us" (50:38). Ayat al-Kursi adds: "neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him" (2:255). The wording of 50:38 contrasts with Exodus 31:17, though it also agrees with Isaiah 40:28 and Psalm 121:4.
  • God "regretted" creating man. Genesis 6:6 says "the LORD regretted that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him to his heart". Yet Numbers 23:19 says "God is not a man… that he should repent", and 1 Samuel 15 says both (v. 11 and 35 he regrets; v. 29 he does not). The Quran's God never regrets: His knowledge encompasses everything before it happens (57:22, 6:59).
  • God defeated by iron chariots. Judges 1:19: "the LORD was with Judah… but they could not drive out the inhabitants of the plain, because they had chariots of iron." The Quran: "If Allah helps you, none can overcome you" (3:160), and "Allah is never to be frustrated by anything in the heavens or the earth" (35:44).
  • God's hand "bound". Lamentations 2:3 says God "drew back His right hand", and later Jewish tradition (3 Enoch 48A; the midrash on Lamentations) describes God's right hand as held behind Him until redemption. The Quran quotes and rejects such a saying: "The Jews say: the hand of Allah is chained. Chained are their hands… rather, both His hands are outstretched; He spends as He wills" (5:64).

Argument 3 of 5

The Quran corrects historical and narrative details

Thesis

In several stories the Quran quietly differs from the Bible on a factual detail, and in each case the Quran's version is the more plausible one.

  1. P1If the Quran merely copied, its details would match its source, including the source's errors.
  2. P2In the Exodus numbers, the Garden story and Zechariah's sign, the Quran deliberately differs, and the difference resolves a problem in the biblical account.

  • The size of the Exodus. Exodus 12:37 says 600,000 men on foot left Egypt, besides women and children, implying two to three million people. Argument 5 of 5

The Torah's own history records loss, rewriting and scribal falsification

Thesis

The Hebrew Bible itself says the book of the law was lost for generations, that scribes falsified it, and that many inspired books are gone, which is exactly what the Quran describes (2:79).

Argument
  1. P1A book that records its own loss and its own scribes' corruption cannot claim perfect preservation.
  2. P2The Hebrew Bible records that the "book of the law" was found in the Temple under King Josiah after it had been forgotten, that the prophet Jeremiah accused the scribes of a "lying pen", and it cites about two dozen works that no longer exist.
  3. ∴By its own testimony, the Torah was not preserved in the way the Quran was, which confirms the Quran's account of the earlier scriptures.
Evidence
  • The book that was found. In 2 Kings 22:8 (2 Chr 34:14), the priest Hilkiah "found the book of the law" in the Temple during repairs, and King Josiah tore his clothes on hearing it because his people had not obeyed it. 2 Kings 23:22 adds that "no such Passover had been kept since the days of the judges". In other words, the book of the law had been lost or neglected before Josiah's time (some scholars think it was newly composed).
  • The lying pen of the scribes. Jeremiah 8:8: "How can you say, 'We are wise, and the law of the LORD is with us'? But behold, the lying pen of the scribes has made it into a lie." The Quran: "Woe to those who write the book with their own hands, then say 'this is from Allah' to buy with it a small price" (2:79). Jeremiah 8:10 even adds the motive: "everyone is greedy for unjust gain".
  • Contradictions about sacrifice. Jeremiah 7:22: "I did not speak to your fathers or command them concerning burnt offerings and sacrifices on the day I brought them out of Egypt"; Amos 5:25 asks, "Did you bring me sacrifices… forty years in the wilderness?" Yet Leviticus is full of sacrificial laws given in the wilderness. Jer 7:22 and Amos 5:25 have been read by some as denying that sacrifice was commanded in the wilderness.
  • Lost inspired books. The Bible cites the Book of the Wars of the LORD (Num 21:14), the Book of Jashar (Josh 10:13; 2 Sam 1:18), the chronicles of Nathan, Gad, Shemaiah and Iddo, and Solomon's 3,000 proverbs and 1,005 songs (1 Kgs 4:32): about two dozen works in all.
  • Ezra and the restoration. Nehemiah 8 shows Ezra reading and re-establishing the law after the Exile; the Jewish apocalypse 4 Ezra 14 even says the books were burned and Ezra rewrote them by inspiration.
  • Modern scholarship. The documentary hypothesis holds that the Torah combines several sources (J, E, D, P), visible for example in the two different orders of creation in Genesis 1 and 2 (Richard Friedman, Who Wrote the Bible?). Many scholars date Deuteronomy to Josiah's time, partly because its curses (Deut 28) follow the pattern of Esarhaddon's 7th-century vassal treaties.

Objections

"Jeremiah 8:8 means the scribes misinterpreted the law, not that they changed the text."

The verse says the pen (Hebrew 'et, a stylus) of the scribes made it a lie, which points to writing. Ellicott's commentary takes it to refer to written work. Either way, a prophet accused the custodians of the law of falsifying it, which is the Quran's charge.

"Lost books were never meant to be scripture."

David ordered the Book of Jashar to be taught (2 Sam 1:18), and Solomon's proverbs and songs were wisdom from God by the Bible's own account. Their loss shows God did not preserve every inspired text of Israel, so "God would never let His word be lost" is not a biblical principle.

"The documentary hypothesis is disputed."

The details are, but that the Torah is composite and was edited over centuries is the mainstream view, and you do not need any particular version to make the point.

Your opponent says

“The Dead Sea Scrolls prove the Hebrew Bible was preserved.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

They show the text was fairly stable from about the 2nd century BCE, but they also contain variant forms (some closer to the Septuagint or Samaritan text), and they come centuries after Moses. Deuteronomy 32:8 in the Scrolls reads "sons of God" where the Masoretic text has "sons of Israel", a theological change.

In one breath

The Bible records that the law was lost and rediscovered, that a prophet condemned the scribes' lying pen, that sacrifice laws were unknown to the prophets, and that many inspired books vanished. This matches the Quran's account in 2:79.
