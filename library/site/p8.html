Women, gender roles and feminism

5 arguments

Feminist objections are the most common opening in a modern debate. The detailed answers on inheritance, women’s testimony, 4:34, polygyny and hijab are already in Part V (Morality in Islam) and are not repeated here. This section covers what those arguments leave open: the claim of equal worth, feminism as a worldview, women in public life, marriage rights, and the customs (honour killing, forced marriage, FGM) that critics blame on Islam. The approach is the same each time. State what the sources say, concede what is true, and put the critic’s own standard under the same scrutiny. A debater who says “that is not in Islam” and is then shown the verse loses the room.

Argument 1 of 5

Equal in worth, different in role

Thesis

The Quran addresses men and women as equals in worth, reward and accountability, and gives them different but complementary roles in some areas. Equality of worth is not the same claim as sameness of every role.

Argument
  1. P1Equality of moral worth and accountability is one claim; identical roles in every institution is a different claim, and the second does not follow from the first.
  2. P2The Quran asserts the first in the plainest terms (33:35; 16:97; 3:195; 49:13), and assigns some roles differently (maintenance in 4:34, shares in 4:11) while attaching matching duties to each.
  3. P3A rule that differentiates roles is unjust only if it is arbitrary or one-sided, and the critic has to show that, not assume it.
  4. ∴Islam teaches equal worth with differentiated roles. Calling that “inequality” already assumes that identical roles are the only fair arrangement, which is a value claim and not a neutral fact.
Evidence
  • Parallel address. 33:35 lists ten qualities (Muslim, believing, obedient, truthful, patient, humble, charitable, fasting, chaste, remembering God) for men and women in matching pairs, and ends with one shared reward: “Allah has prepared for them forgiveness and a great reward.”
  • The same reward. “Whoever does righteousness, male or female, while a believer, We will surely cause him to live a good life, and We will surely give them their reward according to the best of what they used to do” (16:97). And “I will not allow the work of any worker among you, male or female, to be lost; you are of one another” (3:195).
  • No ranking by sex. “We created you from a male and a female… the most honoured of you with God is the most God-conscious” (49:13). The only ranking is taqwa.
  • Eve is not blamed alone. The Quran never makes the woman the cause of the Fall. Adam is named as the one who disobeyed (20:121), and the two say together “Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves” (7:23). Compare 1 Timothy 2:14 (“Adam was not deceived, but the woman was deceived”) and Sirach 25:24 (“From a woman sin had its beginning, and because of her we all die”).
  • The Prophet ﷺ. “Women are the twin halves of men” (Abu Dawud 236, Tirmidhi 113; graded sahih by al-Albani). On 2:228 Ibn Abbas said: “I like to adorn myself for my wife as I like her to adorn herself for me” (Tabari).
Arabic

أَنِّي لَا أُضِيعُ عَمَلَ عَامِلٍ مِّنكُم مِّن ذَكَرٍ أَوْ أُنثَىٰ ۖ بَعْضُكُم مِّن بَعْضٍ

3:195
Objections

“‘Equal but different’ is what every sexist said.”

The test is whether the difference is arbitrary and one-sided. Here each difference comes with a matching duty: the man carries the financial obligation (maintenance, the dowry) while the woman’s property stays entirely her own (4:32). The critic also uses a role-based standard of his own when he accepts separate sports categories or maternity leave, so “different treatment” cannot be the thing that is wrong in itself.

“2:228 says men have a degree over women.”

The same verse opens: “And due to the wives is similar to what is expected of them, in a reasonable manner” (wa lahunna mithlu alladhi ‘alayhinna bil-ma‘ruf), then adds “and men have a degree over them.”

Commentators differ: some link the degree to maintenance and leadership, and Ibn Abbas read it as the husband's forbearance and generosity, none as superior worth. Ibn Abbas, quoted above, read the verse as a mutual duty to treat each other well.

Practice

Your opponent says

“If men and women are really equal, why does Islam not make everything identical?”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Because equal worth does not require identical roles, and no society treats them as the same: every legal system separates some categories by sex or by family role. What matters is whether the rule is fair and whether duties match rights. In Islam the woman’s worth, property, reward and accountability are equal to the man’s (33:35, 4:32), and the extra financial burden falls on him. If you think roles must be identical to be fair, that is a premise you have to defend, not a fact we both accept.

In one breath

Men and women are equal in worth, reward and accountability (33:35, 16:97, 49:13) and have complementary roles with matching duties. “Different” is not “unequal” unless you assume identical roles are the only fair design.

Argument 2 of 5

What feminism got right, and where Islam differs

Thesis

Islam agrees with the core early feminist demands (property, education, consent, protection from abuse) and established most of them in the seventh century. It parts company with parts of later feminism on identical roles, sexual autonomy and the status of the family. A debater should concede the agreement before stating the differences.

Argument
  1. P1“Feminism” covers incompatible positions (liberal, difference, radical, and Muslim feminists), so the first question to ask is which claim is being made.
  2. P2The founding demands were property rights, education, consent to marriage and protection from violence, and Islam’s sources establish each of these.
  3. P3Later claims, such as that gender is wholly a social construct, that sexual freedom is liberation and that family roles are oppression, conflict with Islamic teaching, and a Muslim can say so while affirming women’s rights.
  4. ∴A Muslim can affirm women’s dignity and rights fully without adopting every feminist doctrine as a package, and should be honest where Muslim societies have fallen short of their own sources.
Evidence
  • Property. “For men is a share of what the parents and relatives leave, and for women is a share” (4:7). “For women is a share of what they have earned” (4:32). The dowry belongs to the bride: “Give women their dowries as a gift” (4:4). In England, a married woman’s property passed to her husband until the Married Women’s Property Acts of 1870 and 1882.
  • Education. Women came to the Prophet ﷺ and said men had taken all his time, so he set aside a day for them and taught them (Bukhari 101). Aisha became one of the great teachers of the first generation.
  • The vote, for context. Women got the vote in the United States in 1920, in Britain on equal terms in 1928, in France in 1944 and in Swiss federal elections in 1971.
  • Where Muslim societies fail. Denying daughters their inheritance, their education or a say in marriage contradicts the sources. When the Taliban banned girls’ secondary schooling (2021) and women’s university education (2022), Al-Azhar and the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation criticised the decisions as contrary to Islam.
  • Where Islam differs: sameness. In the story of Mary's birth the Quran quotes her mother saying “the male is not like the female” (3:36), and the Quran elsewhere treats sex as real and meaningful (53:45; see the section on sexuality and gender).
  • Where Islam differs: motherhood. A man asked who most deserved his good company and the Prophet ﷺ said: “Your mother”, three times, “then your father” (Bukhari 5971, Muslim 2548).
  • Where Islam differs: sexual ethics. Sex is lawful only within marriage for both sexes, which is a different understanding of liberation from the one in most Western feminism.
Arabic

وَلَيْسَ الذَّكَرُ كَالْأُنثَىٰ

3:36
Objections

“Muslim feminists like Amina Wadud show Islam can be feminist.”

Some Muslim women scholars have raised real issues about patriarchal readings, and challenging culture presented as religion is legitimate. But when a reading sets aside explicit rulings (Wadud led a mixed-gender Friday prayer in New York in 2005), the great majority of scholars reject it. Distinguish between correcting a cultural reading and discarding a text.

“Women in Muslim countries are oppressed.”

Some are, and often in ways the sources forbid: denying inheritance, schooling or consent. Others are not, and several Muslim-majority countries have elected women as heads of government (Benazir Bhutto in Pakistan in 1988, Tansu Çiller in Turkey, Khaleda Zia and Sheikh Hasina in Bangladesh, Megawati Sukarnoputri as president of Indonesia (2001)). The question is whether the rule is in the sources, not whether every society follows it.

Practice

Your opponent says

“You cannot call yourself pro-woman unless you accept feminism.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Feminism is several different positions, so tell me which one. If you mean property, education, consent and protection from violence, Islam established those in the seventh century (4:7, 4:32, Bukhari 101) when most legal systems did not, and I criticise Muslim societies that deny them. If you mean that sex roles are only a social construct or that sexual freedom is liberation, I disagree and I will say why. Rejecting a package is not the same as rejecting women’s dignity.

In one breath

Islam gave women property, education and consent early, and Muslims should condemn societies that deny them; it differs from parts of later feminism on identical roles, sexual autonomy and the family.

Argument 3 of 5

Women in public life: scholars, traders, advisers and rulers

Thesis

Islamic history shows women as traders, scholars, teachers, advisers, fighters and, rarely, rulers. The main objection, Bukhari 4425, is read by most jurists as restricting the supreme leadership of the community, and other public roles remain open.

Argument
  1. P1A claim that Islam confines women to the home must be tested against the record of the first generations and later centuries.
  2. P2The record shows women in commerce, scholarship, counsel and defence, with the Prophet’s ﷺ approval.
  3. P3The one text that limits a public role speaks about a specific appointment, and scholars differ on how far it extends.
  4. ∴Islam does not keep women out of public life. It limits at most the highest office of the community in the majority view, and Muslim history is full of women exercising authority in knowledge, trade and politics.
Evidence
  • Khadija. A successful merchant who hired Muhammad ﷺ to trade for her before their marriage (Ibn Ishaq), and the first person to believe in him.
  • Umm Salama at Hudaybiyyah. When the Companions hesitated to obey the order to slaughter and shave after the treaty, the Prophet ﷺ followed Umm Salama’s advice, and they obeyed (Bukhari 2731-2732).
  • Aisha. She narrated over two thousand hadith and corrected senior Companions, which al-Zarkashi collected in al-Ijaba li-irad ma istadrakat-hu ‘A’isha ‘ala al-sahaba.
  • Women scholars. Mohammad Akram Nadwi’s al-Muhaddithat documents thousands of women hadith scholars across the centuries. Karima al-Marwaziyya (d. 1070) was one of the most sought-after transmitters of Sahih al-Bukhari.
  • Defenders. Nusayba bint Ka‘b defended the Prophet ﷺ at Uhud (Ibn Ishaq, Ibn Sa‘d).
  • Rulers. Arwa al-Sulayhi ruled Yemen (d. 1138), Shajar al-Durr was sultana of Egypt in 1250, Razia Sultan ruled Delhi in 1236-40, and several sultanas ruled Aceh in the seventeenth century.
  • The hadith. “A people who entrust their affairs to a woman will not prosper” (Bukhari 4425) was said when the Prophet ﷺ learned the Persians had crowned the daughter of Khosrow. The majority hold it restricts the supreme leadership (al-imama al-kubra) to men. Abu Hanifa allowed women as judges in matters where their testimony is accepted, and al-Tabari is reported to have allowed it in all matters.
Objections

“So a woman cannot be head of state in Islam.”

Most classical jurists held that the supreme leadership of the community is for a man, and a few contemporary scholars argue the hadith concerns a specific absolute monarchy. State the majority view and say the disagreement exists. In the modern state, where the head of government is not the caliph, many Muslim scholars and countries accept women in the highest offices.

“Women are told to stay at home (33:33).”

33:33 is addressed to the Prophet’s ﷺ wives, who held a special status (33:32), and the same wives travelled, taught and advised. Women in general prayed in the mosque, traded and taught in the Prophet’s time. The ruling concerns decorum, not exclusion.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Islam locks women in the home.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Look at the record: Khadija was a merchant who employed the Prophet ﷺ, Umm Salama’s advice resolved the crisis at Hudaybiyyah (Bukhari 2731), Aisha taught and corrected Companions, thousands of women were hadith scholars, and queens ruled in Yemen, Egypt and Delhi. There is a majority opinion that the top office of the whole community is for men, based on Bukhari 4425, and I would state that openly. Everything else, from scholarship to commerce, was open.

In one breath

Women were merchants, scholars, advisers, defenders and rulers in Muslim history. The one restriction, from Bukhari 4425, is read by most as limiting the supreme leadership of the community.

Argument 4 of 5

Marriage: consent, the dowry and a wife’s way out

Thesis

In Islamic law a marriage needs the bride’s consent, the dowry is her own property, and she can leave through khul‘ or judicial dissolution. Forced marriage violates the law, and the Prophet ﷺ annulled one.

Argument
  1. P1A marriage contract is valid only with the free consent of both parties.
  2. P2The wife receives a dowry that is hers alone and can stipulate conditions in the contract.
  3. P3She has legal routes out of a marriage she cannot continue.
  4. ∴Islamic marriage law gives the woman consent, property and an exit, and a marriage forced on her is not valid in Islam.
Evidence
  • Consent. “A previously married woman is not to be married until she is consulted, and a virgin is not to be married until her permission is sought.” They asked how a virgin gives permission and he said: “By her silence” (Bukhari 5136).
  • A forced marriage annulled. Khansa bint Khidham’s father married her against her will, and the Prophet ﷺ annulled the marriage (Bukhari 5138). In another report a girl said her father had married her to his nephew; the Prophet ﷺ gave her the choice, and she replied that she accepted but wanted women to know that fathers have no power to force them (Ibn Majah 1874).
  • The dowry. “Give women their dowries as a gift” (4:4). It is the wife’s property and she may spend it as she wishes.
  • Conditions. “The conditions most deserving to be fulfilled are those by which you make intimacy lawful” (Bukhari 2721), which the Hanbali school applies to conditions such as no second wife or no relocation.
  • Khul‘. Thabit ibn Qays's wife (named Habiba bint Sahl in some reports, Jamila in others) told the Prophet ﷺ she did not fault her husband’s character or religion but could not live with him; he asked her to return the garden she had received from him and told Thabit to accept it and divorce her (Bukhari 5273). See also 2:229.
  • Barira. A freed slave woman chose to leave her husband Mughith. The Prophet ﷺ interceded for him, she asked whether he was commanding her, he said he was only interceding, and she declined (Bukhari 5283).
  • Court-ordered divorce. The jurists, especially the Malikis, allow a judge to dissolve a marriage for harm, non-maintenance or desertion.
Objections

“The wali requirement means a woman cannot choose her own husband.”

The majority require a guardian for the marriage as protection and public witness, and that guardian cannot force her against her consent (Bukhari 5136, 5138). The Hanafi school holds that an adult woman may contract her own marriage. The shared principle is that her consent is required.

“Men can divorce by talaq but women cannot.”

The procedures differ: the husband pronounces talaq, and the wife can obtain khul‘ or a judicial dissolution, and can be given the power to divorce herself (tafwid) in the contract. Defend the substance, that she has routes out, and say that in practice courts and customs vary, which is often where abuses occur.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Muslim women are married off against their will.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

That is forced marriage and Islamic law does not accept it. The Prophet ﷺ said a woman must be asked and her permission sought (Bukhari 5136), and when Khansa’s father married her against her will, he annulled it (Bukhari 5138). She also owns her dowry (4:4) and can leave through khul‘ (Bukhari 5273). Where forced marriages happen, they come from custom and they violate the sources.

In one breath

A valid Islamic marriage requires the bride’s consent (Bukhari 5136), the Prophet ﷺ annulled a forced one (5138), the dowry is hers, and she can leave through khul‘ (5273) or the court.

Argument 5 of 5

Honour killing, forced marriage and FGM: customs, not Islamic law

Thesis

Honour killing and forced marriage are forbidden by Islamic law. FGM is not in the Quran, its hadith basis is weak and major Muslim institutions now prohibit it, though some classical jurists recommended or required it. State the record honestly, because the strongest reply concedes what is true.

Argument
  1. P1A practice is Islamic only if the sources and recognised jurists support it, and its prevalence among Muslims does not show that.
  2. P2Killing outside a court is murder in Islamic law, and a charge of unchastity needs four eyewitnesses or a confession.
  3. P3On FGM, the Quran is silent, the hadith evidence is weak and classical opinion was divided, and modern Muslim authorities have prohibited it on the ground of harm.
  4. ∴Honour killings and forced marriage are crimes under Islamic law, and FGM is a disputed custom that Islamic principles of preventing harm now lead major authorities to prohibit.
Evidence
  • Killing. “Whoever kills a soul, unless for a soul or for corruption [done] in the land, it is as if he had slain mankind entirely” (5:32); “Do not kill the soul that Allah has made sacred except by right” (17:33). A father or brother has no right to execute anyone.
  • The standard of proof. Zina requires four eyewitnesses to the act itself (24:13), and a false accusation without them is punished with eighty lashes (24:4). In li‘an a husband who accuses his wife without witnesses must swear oaths, not kill (24:6-9).
  • Not Islamic alone. Honour-based killings are reported among Hindus, Sikhs and Christians too, for example in parts of India, and Italy’s penal code gave reduced sentences for “crimes of honour” until 1981, so they cannot be traced to Islam alone.
  • Forced marriage. Invalid, as in the previous argument (Bukhari 5136, 5138).
  • FGM: the sources. The Quran does not mention it. The report usually cited (Abu Dawud 5271) advises “do not cut severely”, and Abu Dawud himself said that it is weak. The classical schools differ: some Shafi‘i texts call it obligatory, while the Hanafis, Malikis and Hanbalis describe it as a recommended or “honourable” custom for women.
  • FGM: the practice. UNICEF notes that FGM is found among Muslims, Christians and followers of traditional religions in the places where it occurs.
  • Modern rulings. In 2007, after a girl’s death, Egypt’s Grand Mufti Ali Gomaa and Al-Azhar scholars declared the practice forbidden, on the principle “no harm and no reciprocating harm” (Ibn Majah 2341).
Objections

“Muslims do it, so it is Islamic.”

Prevalence shows custom, not doctrine. Honour killings and FGM also occur among non-Muslims, and many Muslim societies do not practise them. The test is what the sources and the scholars say, and on honour killing the sources are clear, while on FGM the better-supported modern position forbids it.

“Some classical scholars made FGM obligatory.”

That is true, and a Muslim should say so. Those opinions were based on a weak report and on the medical knowledge of their time. The jurists’ own principle that harm must be removed is why contemporary bodies, among them Al-Azhar’s scholars and Egypt’s Dar al-Ifta, prohibit it.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Honour killings and genital cutting show what Islam does to women.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

On honour killing, Islamic law calls killing outside a court murder (5:32, 17:33) and demands four eyewitnesses before an accusation of zina (24:4, 24:13), so nothing in the law permits it, and it occurs in other religions too. On FGM, it is not in the Quran, the hadith cited is weak by its own collector’s verdict, some classical jurists did recommend it, and today Egypt’s Grand Mufti and Al-Azhar’s scholars forbid it. I condemn both, and I am telling you what the sources say and where scholars differed.

In one breath

Honour killing and forced marriage are forbidden by Islamic law. FGM has no Quranic basis, a weak hadith, mixed classical opinion and is now prohibited by major authorities; state the history honestly.

Sexuality, gender identity and LGBT questions

4 arguments

This is where most debates now turn hostile, so precision matters more than anywhere else. Islam’s position is a position on acts, it applies to everyone who feels desire outside marriage, and it is held by all four Sunni schools and the Shia schools alike. State it plainly. Do not retreat into vagueness, and do not claim more than the sources say. Two things keep the conversation honest: the difference between a desire and an act, and the difference between what a Muslim believes and what a state may enforce. The Quran’s own words on Lot’s people are the strongest ground you have, so learn them well. Never use dehumanising language, never justify vigilantism, and remember that you are speaking to a person.

Argument 1 of 4

What Islam says, and what it does not

Thesis

Islam prohibits same-sex sexual acts, as Judaism and Christianity historically did, and judges them as acts, not as an identity. The same limit on sexual conduct binds every person, including heterosexuals outside marriage.

Argument
  1. P1Muslims believe God’s guidance includes limits on sexual conduct, and that these limits apply to all people alike.
  2. P2The limit is placed on acts (zina and the act of the people of Lot are both prohibited), not on having an inclination, and heterosexual desire outside marriage is bounded in the same way.
  3. P3Islamic law forbids spying on private conduct and sets very high evidentiary bars, so it gives no licence to hunt people down.
  4. ∴Islam’s position is a universal rule on acts. It is not a verdict that anyone is less than human, and it gives no warrant for harassment or violence.
Evidence
  • The Quran. “Indeed you approach men with desire instead of women. Rather, you are a transgressing people” (7:81). See also 26:165-166, 27:54-55 and 29:28-29.
  • The consensus. The four Sunni schools and the Shia jurists agree the act is forbidden. They differ on penalties: some treat it as a fixed punishment, Abu Hanifa as a discretionary matter (ta‘zir). The report that prescribes death (Abu Dawud 4462) is disputed in grading, so do not build your case on it; the prohibition rests on the Quran and the consensus.
  • Same standard as zina. The same sexual ethic forbids sex outside marriage between a man and a woman, and zina carries a more clearly stated penalty in the Quran (24:2). The rule is about the act and not about the person’s orientation.
  • No spying, high proof. “Do not spy on one another” (49:12). A hadd offence needs a confession or four eyewitnesses to the act itself (24:4, 24:13 for zina), and the Prophet ﷺ urged concealing others’ sins (Bukhari 2442). In practice, private conduct was almost never prosecuted.
  • Vigilantism is forbidden. Only a court may impose a fixed penalty, and a private person who attacks another commits a grave wrong (5:32).
Arabic

إِنَّكُمْ لَتَأْتُونَ الرِّجَالَ شَهْوَةً مِّن دُونِ النِّسَاءِ ۚ بَلْ أَنتُمْ قَوْمٌ مُّسْرِفُونَ

7:81
Objections

“So you think gay people are evil.”

No. Islam does not call a person evil for feeling an attraction; it asks every believer to refrain from certain acts, the unmarried heterosexual included. People are judged on their conduct and their effort, and God alone knows the heart. Mocking, shaming or harming anyone is itself forbidden.

“Your position leads to violence against gay people.”

Violence by individuals is forbidden in Islamic law, and no jurist allows a private person to punish. Where violence occurs it is a crime, as when any group is attacked. A moral belief that a practice is wrong is not an incitement to attack people, and neither is believing that adultery is wrong.

“A Muslim minority in the West should therefore want such acts banned.”

Muslims living in a non-Muslim country are bound by their agreements with their society and have no duty to impose their ethics by force. They can hold a belief and argue for it by persuasion while obeying the law of the land.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Do you really believe that being gay is a sin?”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

I believe Islam forbids same-sex sexual acts, as it forbids all sex outside marriage, and as Judaism and Christianity historically did. It does not say that a person is sinful for an attraction. The rule is about conduct and it applies to everyone. Nobody has the right to harass or harm anyone over it, and Islamic law forbids spying on people’s private lives.

In one breath

Islam forbids same-sex acts as it forbids all sex outside marriage, judges acts and not persons, forbids spying, vigilantism and harassment, and demands very high proof for any legal penalty.

Argument 2 of 4

“Born this way” does not settle the moral question

Thesis

Even if sexual orientation is partly innate, it does not follow that acting on it is permissible. People accept that inclinations they did not choose still may need restraint, and Islam treats such inclinations as a test, not as a verdict.

Argument
  1. P1From the claim that a trait is natural or innate it does not follow that acting on it is right (the is-ought gap).
  2. P2Many predispositions that people did not choose, such as aggression, addiction proneness or attraction outside a committed relationship, are rightly restrained.
  3. P3Islam teaches that God tests people through desires and hardships and does not burden anyone beyond capacity (2:286).
  4. ∴“Born this way” cannot settle whether an act is permitted. What settles it is what God has permitted, and Islam answers that with a rule for acts and a promise of reward for the restraint it asks.
Evidence
  • The is-ought gap. David Hume’s point that moral conclusions do not follow from facts alone is accepted across moral philosophy. Natural does not equal good, and the reverse also fails.
  • The science is not what the slogan claims. A large 2019 genome-wide study of nearly half a million people (Ganna et al., Science) found no single “gay gene”, with common genetic variants together accounting for roughly 8 to 25 percent of the variation in same-sex behaviour. A review by Bailey and colleagues (Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2016) concluded that orientation has multiple biological and non-social influences, with little support for social or learned causes; either way, the moral question is not settled by causes.
  • Islam’s framework. “He who created death and life to test you as to which of you is best in deed” (67:2). “Hell is surrounded by desires and Paradise is surrounded by hardships” (Muslim 2822). “Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity” (2:286).
  • The unmarried. Islam asks every unmarried person to restrain sexual desire. The Prophet ﷺ told young men who could not marry to fast (Bukhari 5066), and the Quran praises Yahya (John) as hasur, one who restrained himself (3:39).
  • Complementarity. The Quran presents the male-female pairing as part of creation: “And of everything We created two mates” (51:49), and marriage as a source of tranquillity (30:21).

لَا يُكَلِّفُ اللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا

2:286
Objections

“If God made them this way, why would He forbid it?”

The same question applies to anyone with a strong desire they are asked to restrain, whether it is for sex outside marriage, for revenge or for another person’s property. The Islamic answer is that life is a test, that restraint is rewarded, and that God does not impose what a person cannot bear (2:286).

“That is cruel. It asks people to be alone.”

Islam asks all unmarried people to refrain until marriage, and for some of them that lasts a lifetime. It values celibacy, forbids shaming those who struggle, and places a heavy duty on the community to support them. The difficulty of a rule is a reason for compassion in applying it, not proof that it is false.

Practice

Your opponent says

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Whether it is chosen is one question and whether it is right to act on it is another. Plenty of things people did not choose, like a quick temper or a desire for many partners, are still things we ask them to restrain. The research shows influences and not a single cause, and it cannot tell us what is permitted. Islam’s answer is that God tests us with desires and does not burden anyone beyond capacity, and that applies to every Muslim, whatever their desire.

In one breath

Innate does not mean permitted: the is-ought gap applies, the science shows influences and no single cause, and Islam treats inclinations as a test that every believer faces and that God does not make unbearable.

Argument 3 of 4

The revisionist reading of Lot’s people

Thesis

Some claim the story of Lot is about rape or inhospitality and not about same-sex relations. The Quran’s own wording rules that out, and every major classical commentary reads it the same way.

Argument
  1. P1If the sin were only violence or inhospitality, the rebuke would be about violence or inhospitality.
  2. P2The Quran’s rebuke names the act itself: approaching men “with desire instead of women” (7:81) and leaving “what your Lord has created for you of your wives” (26:165-166).
  3. P3A reading that has to ignore these phrases is reading the present into the text.
  4. ∴The text identifies same-sex relations as the offence, together with other crimes of the people, and the revisionist reading does not survive the wording.
Evidence
  • The wording. “Do you approach males among the worlds and leave what your Lord has created for you of your wives? Rather, you are a people transgressing” (26:165-166). “You approach men with desire instead of women” (7:81). The Quran calls it fahisha, “an immorality no one in the worlds preceded you in” (7:80).
  • The revisionist point. 29:29 adds that they “cut the road” and commit “evil in your gatherings”, and revisionists argue that the real crime was highway robbery and assault. But the verse begins with the act (“Do you approach men…?”) and lists other crimes in addition to it, so the act is not erased.
  • Lot’s offer. “These are my daughters; they are purer for you” (11:78; compare 15:71). The commentators (Tabari, Ibn Kathir) read this as an appeal to lawful marriage, which makes sense only if the issue is the kind of relation sought, not rape of anyone.
  • Across scripture. The same story and the same prohibition appear in Genesis 19, Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13 and Jude 7. Muslims, Jews and Christians read these as condemning the act for most of their history.
  • The revisionists. The best-known contemporary argument is Scott Siraj al-Haqq Kugle’s Homosexuality in Islam (2010). Kugle argues from philological and hadith analysis that the Lot narrative concerns rape, inhospitality and violence, and mainstream scholars in every school reject that reading because it sets aside the explicit wording.
Objections

“The Quran never mentions lesbians or consensual same-sex love.”

The Quran’s statements on this are addressed to the act in general terms, and the jurists derived the prohibition from them and from the Prophetic rulings on sexual conduct. The absence of a modern category in a seventh-century text is not an absence of a ruling on the act.

“Kugle is a scholar and you are not.”

He is entitled to his views, but the Quranic text he is reinterpreting can be read by anyone who reads Arabic, and the phrase “instead of women” cannot be explained as a reference to rape. A scholar’s argument must be judged on whether it fits the evidence, and this one does not.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Lot’s people were destroyed for rape and inhospitality, not for being gay.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Read what God says to them: “You approach men with desire instead of women” (7:81) and “Do you approach males and leave what your Lord created for you of your wives?” (26:165-166). Tabari, Qurtubi and Ibn Kathir all read it this way, and so did Jews and Christians reading the same story.

In one breath

The Quran names the act (7:81, 26:165-166), the other crimes in 29:29 are additional and not a replacement, and every major commentary reads the story the same way.

Argument 4 of 4

Sex, gender and the categories Islamic law uses

Thesis

Islamic law treats male and female as real and meaningful categories, recognises the rare intersex condition (khuntha) with detailed rulings, and forbids imitation of the other sex. A person’s felt identity is not treated as changing their legal sex, though scholars differ on some medical questions.

Argument
  1. P1Law needs categories, and Islamic law has long had a category for the exceptional case of ambiguous sex.
  2. P2The existence of a recognised exception (khuntha) presupposes, and does not erase, the normal male-female binary.
  3. P3The Prophet ﷺ cursed men who imitate women and women who imitate men.
  4. ∴Islam’s framework is a binary with a defined exception for intersex people, and it rejects the idea that gender is self-defined. Contemporary rulings on surgery differ and should be stated as differing, not as a single position.
Evidence
  • The categories. “He created the two mates, the male and the female” (53:45). Classical fiqh devotes chapters to the khuntha, the person of ambiguous sex, with rulings on prayer rows, inheritance and marriage, and the jurists assign the person to a sex by physical signs wherever these exist, which presupposes the two categories.
  • The hadith. “The Messenger of Allah ﷺ cursed men who imitate women and women who imitate men” (Bukhari 5885, from Ibn Abbas).
  • The mukhannathun. The Prophet ﷺ ordered the effeminate men (mukhannathun) who had been allowed into women's quarters to be kept out after one described a woman's body (Bukhari 5235); the report is also cited by jurists in discussing gender behaviour.
  • Medical rulings differ. Contemporary scholars generally allow surgery to correct a physical ambiguity in intersex people. Rulings on sex reassignment for people without a physical ambiguity differ: many bodies, including councils of fiqh scholars, forbid it, while a few authorities (for example, a 1980s ruling associated with Ayatollah Khomeini in Iran) permitted it in some cases.
  • Pastoral note. Islamic law protects the dignity of all people, forbids insults, and requires equal justice in courts, whatever a person’s status.
Objections

“Intersex people prove sex is not binary.”

Intersex conditions are rare variations in development, and classical Islamic law, like modern medicine, treats them as exceptions needing their own rulings. A rare exception does not turn a binary into a spectrum, just as the existence of people born with six fingers does not mean humans have a variable number of fingers.

“Different Muslim scholars disagree about transition, so Islam has no position.”

They disagree on the medical question of surgery, but they do not disagree that God created male and female and that men and women should not imitate each other’s sex. Distinguish a disagreement on details from the absence of any position.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Gender is an identity, not biology. Who are you to say what anyone is?”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Islamic law works with male and female as real categories, and has always had a special rule for the rare intersex person, which only makes sense if the two categories are real. Muslims believe that is how God created humanity (53:45). That does not mean anyone may mock or mistreat another person. Muslim scholars differ on questions of surgery, and I would tell you that honestly, but none of them says identity alone changes someone’s legal sex.

In one breath

Islamic law recognises male, female and the rare intersex exception, forbids imitation of the other sex (Bukhari 5885), and does not treat self-defined identity as changing legal sex; rulings on surgery differ.

Liberalism, secularism and human rights

5 arguments

Most modern debates are not Islam against “neutral reason”. They are Islam against another worldview, secular liberalism, which presents itself as neutral. This section gives you the tools to show that politely and with sources: that liberalism rests on contested premises, that universal rights need a ground, that “religion causes wars” does not survive the data, and that Muslims can be loyal citizens of secular states, and that Islam’s teaching on consultation is more nuanced than “Islam versus democracy”. (Minorities under Muslim rule are covered in Part V, Morality in Islam.) Use the strongest sources the other side respects (Rawls, Habermas, Cavanaugh, Rorty), and concede what is true: liberal societies protect real goods, and Muslim societies have a mixed record.

Argument 1 of 5

Secular liberalism is a worldview, not a neutral referee

Thesis

Secular liberalism claims to be neutral between ways of life, but its tests for what counts as harm, consent and personhood depend on disputed views of the good. It is one worldview among others and has to argue its case like the rest.

Argument
  1. P1A procedure can be neutral only if its basic standards do not favour any side in the dispute it settles.
  2. P2The liberal tests (the harm principle, consent, autonomy, dignity) each need an answer to contested questions: what counts as harm, who counts as a person, and what makes a life good.
  3. P3Liberal states themselves restrict consensual acts on grounds of dignity or morality.
  4. ∴Liberalism cannot claim the neutral high ground. The real debate is between comprehensive views, and Islam can present its own case on equal terms.
Evidence
  • Philosophers admit it. Alasdair MacIntyre (After Virtue, 1981; Whose Justice? Which Rationality?, 1988) argued that there is no tradition-independent standpoint of reason. Michael Sandel (Liberalism and the Limits of Justice, 1982) argued that liberalism presupposes a particular picture of the self. John Rawls himself retreated in Political Liberalism (1993) to claim only a limited, “political” neutrality.
  • Mill’s test needs a view of harm. John Stuart Mill’s harm principle (On Liberty, 1859) allows interference only to prevent harm to others. But what counts as harm (offence? damage to the social fabric? harm to the self?) is exactly what people dispute.
  • Consent is not enough even for liberal states. In 1995 France’s Conseil d’État upheld the ban on dwarf-tossing despite the consent of the dwarf, on grounds of human dignity (Commune de Morsang-sur-Orge). In Stübing v. Germany (2012) the European Court of Human Rights upheld a criminal ban on consensual incest between adult siblings. Both restrict consenting adults on moral grounds.
  • Quran 2:256. Islam does not claim to coerce belief (2:256; 10:99), and so argues its case in public. The Quran invites evidence: “Bring your proof, if you are truthful” (2:111).
Objections

“Liberalism at least lets everyone live as they choose.”

It protects real goods, and Muslims benefit from freedom of conscience in liberal states. But it does not let everyone live as they choose: it forbids polygamy, drug sale, certain consensual acts and some speech. Every society draws lines, and liberalism draws its lines using its own contested values.

“Religious claims need justification; liberal values do not.”

Both do. Appealing to “harm” or “autonomy” without an account of why those are the right values is as much a faith commitment as appealing to revelation. The debate should compare grounds, not exempt one side from giving them.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Secular law is neutral. Religious law imposes one group’s morals on everyone.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

All law imposes someone’s morals. France bans dwarf-tossing with the dwarf’s consent, and the European Court upheld a ban on consensual adult incest. Those are moral judgements about dignity. Rawls himself accepted that liberalism could claim only a limited, political neutrality. So the real question is which moral foundations are true, and I am glad to compare grounds.

In one breath

Liberalism’s tests for harm, consent and personhood rest on disputed moral views, and liberal states restrict consensual acts on moral grounds. It is a worldview to be argued for, not a neutral referee.

Argument 2 of 5

Where do universal human rights come from?

Thesis

Universal, equal human rights need a ground that makes every person’s worth equal and non-negotiable. Some leading secular thinkers acknowledge the religious roots of equal human dignity (Habermas), argue that Locke's equality rested on theology (Waldron), or deny that rights need any foundation (Rorty), while Islam supplies one in creation and honour from God.

Argument
  1. P1A claim that every human has equal, inalienable worth needs a reason that does not depend on abilities, since abilities differ.
  2. P2Secular accounts of the ground of rights are contested, and some of their best defenders admit the difficulty.
  3. P3The Quran grounds human worth in God’s act of honouring humanity, independent of status.
  4. ∴Islam supplies a ground for universal human dignity that is stronger than a social convention, and the secular critic must explain what grounds rights in its place.
Evidence
  • Habermas. The German philosopher Jürgen Habermas, a non-believer, wrote that universalistic egalitarianism, “from which sprang the ideas of freedom and social solidarity”, is “the direct legacy of the Judaic ethic of justice and the Christian ethic of love” (Time of Transitions, 2006).
  • Waldron and Wolterstorff. Jeremy Waldron (God, Locke, and Equality, 2002) argues that Locke’s basic equality depends on theological premises. Nicholas Wolterstorff (Justice: Rights and Wrongs, 2008) argues that rights are grounded in being loved by God.
  • Rorty’s alternative. Richard Rorty (“Human Rights, Rationality, and Sentimentality”, 1993) argued that rights rest on no deep foundation, only on cultivated sentiment, a view Rorty held to be sufficient, and which critics say leaves rights without a ground.
  • The Islamic ground. “We have certainly honoured the children of Adam” (17:70). “We created you from a male and female… the most honoured is the most God-conscious” (49:13). “Whoever kills a soul… it is as if he had slain mankind entirely” (5:32). The Prophet ﷺ declared in the Farewell Sermon that “your blood, your wealth and your honour are sacred to each other” (Bukhari 1739, Muslim 1218), and that no Arab has superiority over a non-Arab except by taqwa (Ahmad 23489).
Arabic

وَلَقَدْ كَرَّمْنَا بَنِي آدَمَ

17:70
Objections

“Atheists can be good without God.”

Of course; the argument is about grounding and not about behaviour. The question is not whether atheists behave well but what makes human equality true, rather than a useful convention. A convention can be changed by a majority, while a right grounded in God cannot.

“Religion has been used to deny rights.”

That is true of every ideology that has held power, secular ones included. It shows that a ground can be misused. It does not remove the need for a ground, and it does not tell us whether the Islamic ground in 17:70 and 49:13 is true.

Your opponent says

“Human rights do not need God. They are common sense.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

If they were common sense, there would be no need for the philosophy. Habermas, who is not religious, concedes that universal egalitarianism is the legacy of Jewish and Christian ethics, and Rorty admits rights rest on sentiment, not a foundation. In Islam the ground is that God honoured every child of Adam (17:70) and that only taqwa ranks people (49:13). I am asking what grounds rights for you.

In one breath

Equal, universal rights need a ground. Habermas concedes their religious lineage, Rorty finds no deeper foundation, and the Quran grounds dignity in God’s honouring of humanity (17:70, 49:13).

Argument 3 of 5

“Religion causes most wars” and the data

Thesis

The claim that religion is the main cause of war is not supported by the historical record, and the category “religious violence” is itself contested. This is not a defence of every act done in religion’s name.

Argument
  1. P1A causal claim about the cause of most wars must be supported by a classification of wars.
  2. P2The best-known classification of wars in the standard reference work finds a small share with religion as the primary cause.
  3. P3The twentieth century’s largest atrocities were committed by states and ideologies that were avowedly secular.
  4. ∴“Religion causes most wars” is a slogan. The honest position is that people have used religion, nationalism, ideology and economics as causes and justifications, and none of them has a clean record.
Evidence
  • The Encyclopedia of Wars. Charles Phillips and Alan Axelrod’s Encyclopedia of Wars (2005) records 1,763 wars, of which 123 (about 7 percent) are classified as religious. Be honest about the rest of the figure: the 123 figure is taken from the encyclopedia's index, and secondary tallies put about 66 of them as involving Islam; the encyclopedia does not itself make that classification. The coding is also debated, so cite it as a rough measure.
  • The category itself. William Cavanaugh (The Myth of Religious Violence, 2009) argues that the line between “religious” and “secular” violence is an arbitrary modern construction, and that nationalism and liberal ideology function as quasi-religions that also command killing and sacrifice.
  • The twentieth century. Estimates put the deaths of the Second World War at around 70 to 85 million, and the Soviet and Chinese famines and purges at tens of millions more, under states that officially rejected religion or acted on nationalist and racial ideology.
  • Islam’s own rules. “Do not transgress; indeed Allah does not like transgressors” (2:190). “Whoever kills a soul unjustly… it is as if he had killed all mankind” (5:32). Abu Bakr’s instructions to his armies forbade killing women, children, the elderly and monks and cutting down fruit trees (Muwatta Malik, Book of Jihad, a mursal report widely cited by jurists).
Objections

“But what about ISIS and Muslim terrorism?”

Acknowledge it and condemn it. Groups that kill civilians violate the Quran’s rules (5:32) and the Prophet’s instructions in war, and the great majority of Muslim scholars and the victims of such groups are themselves Muslims. Naming Islam as the cause requires showing the groups’ conduct follows the sources, and it does not.

“The 7 percent figure is only for wars labelled religious.”

That is the point of the coding dispute: if nationalism and ideology are also treated as secular belief systems, the picture shifts further away from religion as a special cause. The honest claim is that the evidence does not support the slogan, not that religion is harmless.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Religion is the biggest cause of war in history.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

That is not what the data shows. In the Encyclopedia of Wars, 123 of 1,763 wars are classified as religious, about 7 percent, and I should add that 66 of those involve Islam, so I am not hiding anything. William Cavanaugh also argues that the line between religious and secular violence is arbitrary. The deadliest conflicts of the last century were fought by nations and ideologies that were openly secular. And Islam’s rules of war forbid harming civilians (5:32).

In one breath

About 7 percent of 1,763 wars are classified as religious (66 of 123 involving Islam), the category is contested, the largest modern atrocities were secular, and Islam’s own rules forbid killing civilians.

Argument 4 of 5

Muslims as citizens of secular states

Thesis

Classical and contemporary jurists broadly hold that Muslims living in a non-Muslim state are bound by their agreements and by the law of the land wherever it does not require disobeying God. The charge that sharia makes Muslims disloyal confuses personal religious practice with imposing penal law.

Argument
  1. P1Loyalty is shown by obligations kept, not by which legal system one would design.
  2. P2Islamic law treats entry into and residence in a country as a covenant that must be honoured, and the Prophet’s ﷺ own followers lived under a Christian king.
  3. P3The penal rulings of Islamic law presuppose an Islamic court and state, so no individual or community living elsewhere is authorised to enforce them.
  4. ∴A Muslim can be a loyal citizen of a secular state, keep the law, and practise and argue for his religion by persuasion and lawful means. Say this plainly, and say that some scholars are stricter on points like naturalisation.
Evidence
  • Abyssinia. The Prophet ﷺ sent persecuted Muslims to the Christian king of Abyssinia, “a king in whose land no one is wronged” (the account of Ja‘far ibn Abi Talib, Ahmad 1740). They lived there for years under his protection without rebelling against it.
  • Covenants. “Fulfil the covenant; indeed the covenant will be questioned” (17:34). “Muslims are bound by their conditions” (Abu Dawud 3594, Tirmidhi 1352). “Whoever kills a person under covenant will not smell the fragrance of Paradise” (Bukhari 3166).
  • Hudhayfa at Badr. Hudhayfa ibn al-Yaman and his father promised the Quraysh not to fight alongside the Prophet ﷺ in return for being let go. The Prophet ﷺ told them to keep the promise: “We will fulfil their covenant and seek Allah’s help against them” (Muslim 1787).
  • Jurists on minorities. Contemporary scholars of the “fiqh of minorities”, such as Taha Jabir al-Alwani and the European Council for Fatwa and Research (founded 1997), rule that Muslims in the West should obey the law of the land and honour the covenant of residence. The old division of the world into dar al-Islam and dar al-harb is a juristic construct from a period of constant war, not a Quranic category, and al-Shafi‘i is usually credited with recognising a third category of covenant (dar al-‘ahd / dar al-sulh).
  • Where scholars differ. A minority of scholars, especially in some Salafi circles, discourage naturalisation or participation in politics in non-Muslim states. A debater should say that the majority view is the one above.
Objections

“Muslims want sharia to replace our laws.”

Most Muslims in secular states want the freedom to pray, fast, eat halal and dress modestly, which is religious freedom, and they argue for their views by lawful means like any other group. Islamic penal law requires an Islamic court and state, and no jurist authorises individuals in a non-Muslim country to apply it. A person can hold that God’s law is best and also keep his covenant with his neighbours.

“An oath of allegiance conflicts with allegiance to God.”

Most scholars say a citizenship oath can be taken where it does not require disobeying God, in the same way that Muslims honour contracts and visas. Some stricter scholars object. The majority position is the one most Muslims in the West follow.

Islamic law tells Muslims to keep their covenants (17:34, Abu Dawud 3594), and the Prophet ﷺ sent his followers to live under a Christian king. In one breath

Muslims in non-Muslim states are bound by their covenants (17:34), the Prophet ﷺ sent Muslims to live under a Christian king, and most contemporary jurists tell them to obey the law of the land.

Argument 5 of 5

Shura, sovereignty and the “Islam versus democracy” charge

Thesis

Islam has no clergy or priestly rule, requires consultation in public affairs, and places ultimate sovereignty with God. Whether this is compatible with democracy depends on definitions, and Muslim thinkers differ.

Argument
  1. P1To answer the charge you must say what is meant by democracy: popular participation, or ultimate popular sovereignty over every question.
  2. P2Islam commands consultation (42:38, 3:159) and has no priesthood with authority to legislate on its own.
  3. P3Islam holds that the limits of the permitted and forbidden are God’s, which clashes with a doctrine of unlimited popular sovereignty.
  4. ∴Islam is compatible with participation, consultation and accountability, and in tension with the view that a majority can decide every question. Say so, and show where Muslim scholars differ.
Evidence
  • Consultation. “And whose affair is consultation among themselves” (42:38) describes the believers. “And consult them in the matter” (3:159) was addressed to the Prophet ﷺ himself.
  • No priesthood. Islam has no sacramental clergy. Scholars (‘ulama) hold authority by knowledge and can be challenged; the caliphs Abu Bakr and Umar were publicly corrected by ordinary Muslims, and the rulers were accountable to the law.
  • Early practice. Abu Bakr was chosen at the gathering at Saqifa and then given the public pledge (bay‘a), and Umar appointed a consultative council for his succession. These show consultation in practice, though not a modern electoral system.
  • Where Muslim thinkers differ. Some (such as Abul A‘la Mawdudi) stressed God’s sovereignty (hakimiyya) as opposed to popular sovereignty; others (such as Rashid al-Ghannushi and many contemporary scholars) argue that democratic institutions are a way to implement consultation and accountability within Islamic limits.
Arabic

وَأَمْرُهُمْ شُورَىٰ بَيْنَهُمْ

42:38
Objections

“A system with divine limits is not a democracy.”

It is not a system of unlimited majority rule, and Islam says so. Many constitutional democracies also have limits that majorities cannot cross, such as entrenched rights. The question is which limits and who decides, and Islam places the limits in revelation, with consultation in everything else.

“Muslim countries are not democratic.”

Many are authoritarian, and Muslim societies need accountability. That is a fact about governments, and several Muslim-majority states hold elections. Judge the doctrine by its texts and the competing scholarly views, not only by the worst governments.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Islam and democracy cannot go together.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

It depends on what you mean. If democracy is consultation, participation and accountable rulers, the Quran commands consultation (42:38) and the early caliphs were chosen by pledge and publicly corrected. If it means a majority can decide any question, including what God has forbidden, then Islam differs, and so do many constitutional democracies that limit what majorities can do. Muslim thinkers differ among themselves on this, and I would show you where.

In one breath

Islam commands consultation (42:38), has no priesthood and holds rulers accountable, and puts ultimate limits with God. Compatibility with democracy depends on the definition, and Muslim thinkers disagree.

Apostasy and blasphemy

4 arguments

These are the questions where evasion is most costly. The majority classical position on apostasy is capital punishment after a call to repent, and several Muslim-majority countries have blasphemy laws with severe penalties. If you pretend otherwise, the first search result will refute you. The sound approach has three parts. First, state the classical ruling accurately and say who may apply it. Second, show what the sources actually say about belief, compulsion and the Prophet’s own conduct, including where scholars dissent. Third, keep the comparison fair: every society limits some speech and punishes betrayal in time of war. Condemn mob violence clearly, since Islamic law gives no private person the right to punish.

Argument 1 of 4

What the classical law says on apostasy, stated honestly

Thesis

The majority of classical jurists held that a Muslim who publicly abandons Islam is to be called to repent and then, if he refuses, faces the death penalty from a court. There are early and modern dissenting views. A debater should state this plainly before giving context.

Argument
  1. P1You cannot defend or explain a ruling you have misstated.
  2. P2The hadith and the practice of the early schools support a capital penalty for apostasy after repentance is offered, as the mainstream position.
  3. P3A continuous line of scholars, early and contemporary, restricts or rejects the penalty, and the penalty is for courts, not individuals.
  4. ∴State the majority ruling accurately, say who may apply it and under what conditions, and then show the debate within the tradition. This gives you credibility and keeps the discussion on the real issues.
Evidence
  • The hadith. “Whoever changes his religion, kill him” (Bukhari 6922). And: “The blood of a Muslim is not lawful except for one of three: a life for a life, a married adulterer, and the one who leaves his religion and separates from the community” (Bukhari 6878, Muslim 1676). The second wording ties the offence to leaving and “separating from the community”.
  • The schools. The majority hold that the apostate is called to repent first. The Hanafis hold that women are imprisoned and not killed, while the other schools include women. The Malikis give three days’ delay.
  • Umar’s reaction. When told that an apostate had been killed straight away, Umar said: “Why did you not imprison him for three days, give him a loaf every day, and call him to repent? Perhaps he would have repented and Allah would have accepted him” (Muwatta Malik).
  • Dissent. Ibrahim al-Nakha‘i and Sufyan al-Thawri held that the apostate should be invited to repent indefinitely. In modern times Mahmud Shaltut and Taha Jabir al-Alwani (La ikraha fi al-din) argued that the penalty is discretionary or limited to those who fight the community, and Abdullah Saeed and Hassan Saeed wrote Freedom of Religion, Apostasy and Islam (2004) in the same direction.
  • Who applies it. Fixed penalties are for a judge in a functioning Islamic court, never for individuals, and a Muslim minority living in a non-Muslim state is not authorised to apply them.
Objections

“So you want to kill people who leave Islam?”

No Muslim in a non-Muslim society is allowed to punish anyone, and no individual in any society may do so. The classical law assigns the decision to a court and has the conditions above. The question of whether and how it should be applied in modern states is debated among Muslim scholars, as the dissenting views show. I would rather give you the accurate position than deny it.

“Your dissenters are a minority.”

They are a minority on the question of punishment, and I said so. But they are classical as well as modern, and their argument is based on the sources, the Quran’s silence on any earthly penalty and the Prophet’s own practice (see the next two arguments).

Practice

Your opponent says

“Islam says apostates must be executed. Is that true or not?”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

The majority of classical jurists said yes, after a call to repent, and carried out by a court, based on Bukhari 6922 and the hadith of the three cases (Bukhari 6878). I won’t deny it. But there is a long tradition of dissent from al-Nakha‘i and al-Thawri to Shaltut and al-Alwani, Umar objected to a man’s execution without a chance to repent, and the Quran itself names no earthly penalty. So the honest answer is: that is the majority classical ruling, and it is debated.

In one breath

The majority classical ruling is death for apostasy after a call to repent, by a court. There is early and modern dissent, no private person may apply it, and a debater should say all three.

Argument 2 of 4

“No compulsion in religion” and the apostasy ruling

Thesis

The Quran guarantees freedom to choose belief, never prescribes an earthly penalty for apostasy, and the classical ruling is linked to public defection from the community. The tension is real and the answers are scholarly, not rhetorical.

Argument
  1. P1Entering Islam is not by force, which the Quran states repeatedly.
  2. P2The Quran speaks of apostasy only with consequences in the hereafter.
  3. P3The classical jurists treated apostasy as a public act with consequences for the community, which is why they often compare it to treason.
  4. ∴The ruling cannot be derived from the Quran’s text on belief alone, and defenders must give the full reasoning, including its disputed parts, rather than quoting 2:256 as if it settled the matter.
Evidence
  • Freedom of belief. “There is no compulsion in religion; the right path has become clear from the wrong” (2:256). “Would you then compel the people until they become believers?” (10:99). “Say: the truth is from your Lord, so whoever wills let him believe, and whoever wills let him disbelieve” (18:29). “You are only a reminder; you are not a controller over them” (88:21-22).
  • The Quran’s verses on apostasy. 2:217, 3:90, 4:137 and 16:106 speak of loss in this world and the next and a severe punishment from God, but none prescribes an earthly penalty.
  • The treason analogy. Many classical jurists linked the death penalty to apostasy together with leaving the community (the second wording of the hadith in Muslim 1676). Most states treat betrayal in wartime harshly: treason can carry death under US federal law (18 U.S.C. § 2381), and wartime desertion was punished by execution (Private Eddie Slovik in 1945).
  • The limits of the analogy. A critic will say that belief is not treason. The classical argument is that in the early community religion was the political identity, so public renunciation was treated as defection. Whether that logic applies now is exactly what contemporary scholars dispute.
Arabic

لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ ۖ قَدْ تَبَيَّنَ الرُّشْدُ مِنَ الْغَيِّ

2:256
Objections

“You cannot have both 2:256 and a death penalty.”

The verse guarantees that nobody is forced in, and Islamic law has historically distinguished a private change of belief, which jurists said could not be investigated, from public renunciation. The ruling is a point of dispute inside the tradition, and I have not claimed that it fully resolves the tension; I have said where the debate lies.

“Treason is about betraying the state, not changing your mind.”

That is the strongest objection and the reason many modern scholars reject the penalty for private belief. The classical position is a claim about a particular historical polity, and modern jurists such as al-Alwani argue the reasoning does not carry over.

Practice

Your opponent says

“How can Islam say there is no compulsion in religion and then execute apostates?”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

The Quran does say there is no compulsion (2:256), and it names no earthly punishment for apostasy. The classical jurists attached the penalty to public defection from the community, comparing it to treason, and many modern scholars say that reasoning no longer applies. I admit there is a tension and that scholars dispute how to resolve it, but I would not say the rule is simply a command to force belief.

In one breath

The Quran guarantees no compulsion (2:256) and names no earthly penalty for apostasy. The classical ruling rests on hadith and public defection, and modern scholars dispute whether it still applies.

Argument 3 of 4

The Prophet’s own record with those who left

Thesis

The Prophet’s ﷺ recorded handling of people who left Islam, or were suspected of insincere belief, was largely restraint, and the people executed were tied to murder, theft or armed rebellion.

Argument
  1. P1The Prophet’s ﷺ practice is the best evidence of how the rulings were applied.
  2. P2In many recorded cases of apostasy or hypocrisy he did not punish the person.
  3. P3The cases of execution involved violence, desertion in war or armed rebellion, not belief alone.
  4. ∴The Prophet’s own record supports reading the penalty as tied to violence and betrayal and does not show a general policy of killing people for belief. This is the strongest historical support for the dissenting view, and it is used with the honest note that the majority of jurists read the hadith more broadly.
Evidence
  • The Bedouin who left. A Bedouin came to the Prophet ﷺ, pledged allegiance, fell ill, and asked to withdraw it. The Prophet declined three times, and the man left Medina; no action was taken (Bukhari 7211).
  • Abdullah ibn Ubayy. The leader of the hypocrites was not executed, though Umar asked, because the Prophet ﷺ said: “People would say that Muhammad kills his companions” (Bukhari 4905, Muslim 2584).
  • Abdullah ibn Sa‘d ibn Abi Sarh. A scribe who apostatised and fled to Mecca, then was pardoned at the conquest after Uthman’s intercession (Abu Dawud 2683).
  • Ubaydullah ibn Jahsh. He left Islam for Christianity in Abyssinia, and the sources report no action against him (Ibn Ishaq).
  • Hudaybiyyah. The treaty of Hudaybiyyah required Muslims to send back anyone from Quraysh who came over without his guardian’s permission, while Quraysh was not required to return anyone who went from the Muslims to them. The Prophet ﷺ accepted this and said: “Whoever goes from us to them, may Allah keep him far away” (Muslim 1784). He did not order such people pursued.
  • Those who were executed. The ‘Urayna group accepted Islam, killed a shepherd, mutilated him and stole the camels; some narrations add that they left Islam, and the punishment is usually argued to be for the murder and robbery (Bukhari 233, 3018, 6802). The Ridda wars involved both tribes that withheld zakat and tribes and claimants who abandoned Islam and took up arms; classical sources use the word apostasy, and modern writers dispute how much was political.
Objections

“Bukhari 6922 says kill him. You are ignoring it.”

I am not ignoring it; it is the main proof of the majority view and I have set it out. The question is how the Prophet ﷺ applied his own words, and the record shows restraint and punishment tied to violence or rebellion. The majority jurists read the hadith as general; the dissenters read it in light of this record.

“Those pardoned were exceptions.”

They are the recorded cases. If belief alone were a capital offence enforced without exception, the exceptions would be the rule. That the Prophet ﷺ gave reasons (such as avoiding the label ‘Muhammad kills his companions’) shows he considered the effect on the community.

Practice

Your opponent says

“The Prophet ordered people killed for leaving Islam.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

The recorded cases show otherwise. A Bedouin withdrew his pledge and was let go (Bukhari 7211), Ibn Ubayy was left alone (Bukhari 4905), Abdullah ibn Sa‘d was pardoned (Abu Dawud 2683), and at Hudaybiyyah the Prophet accepted that people who went to Quraysh would not be pursued (Muslim 1784). The group executed in Bukhari 233 had killed and robbed. The majority jurists read Bukhari 6922 more broadly, and I would tell you that.

In one breath

The Prophet ﷺ let the Bedouin go, spared Ibn Ubayy, pardoned Abdullah ibn Sa‘d and accepted the Hudaybiyyah clause; those executed had killed or rebelled. Majority jurists still read the hadith more broadly.

Argument 4 of 4

Blasphemy: classical rulings, patience, and the free-speech comparison

Thesis

Classical law treats public insult of the Prophet ﷺ as a grave offence, schools differ on the penalty and on non-Muslims, and the Quran and Sunnah command patience under insult. Modern statutes are often broader than the classical law, and mob action has no basis in it.

Argument
  1. P1Every society limits some speech, and the question is where and why.
  2. P2Classical Islamic law and the Prophet’s own conduct under insult are more nuanced than the modern headlines suggest.
  3. P3Vigilante violence over blasphemy has no basis in Islamic law.
  4. ∴Islam treats insult of the sacred as serious, commands patience in response to personal abuse, leaves penalties to a court, and applies no more of a double standard than states that restrict speech they find intolerable.
Evidence
  • The Quran’s tone. “Do not insult those they invoke besides Allah, lest they insult Allah in enmity without knowledge” (6:108). “Repel evil by that which is better” (41:34). “When you hear the verses of Allah denied and mocked, do not sit with them” (4:140), which prescribes leaving the gathering, not violence.
  • The Prophet’s patience. When some Jews greeted him with “al-sam ‘alaykum” (death be upon you), Aisha answered in kind and he said: “Gently, Aisha” (Bukhari 6024). At Ta’if the angel of the mountains offered to crush the town, and he refused (Bukhari 3231).
  • Ka‘b ibn al-Ashraf. The best-known case of a man killed for insulting the Prophet ﷺ (Bukhari 4037) is one where he was also inciting Quraysh to war, breaking the Medina covenant, and composing verses against Muslim women. Classical jurists differ on whether the case shows a rule on insult alone.
  • The schools. The Malikis, Shafi‘is and Hanbalis (see Ibn Taymiyya’s al-Sarim al-maslul) hold that insulting the Prophet ﷺ merits death. The early Hanafi position is that a non-Muslim who insults is not put to death for it, and that a Muslim who does so is an apostate, though later Hanafi jurists allowed discretionary death for repeat offences. The details of repentance also differ.
  • Modern statutes. Pakistan’s section 295-C was introduced in 1986, and the Federal Shariat Court in 1990 held that death should be the only penalty. Accusations there are frequently used to settle personal and property disputes, and mob killings (Mashal Khan in 2017) and the assassination of Salmaan Taseer in 2011 show the danger. These are not required by classical law, and many Pakistani scholars condemn them.
  • The comparison. England’s blasphemy law was only abolished in 2008, the last major case being Whitehouse v Lemon (1979); Ireland held a referendum in 2018 and abolished the offence by statute in 2019 (in force January 2020). Germany (§130 of its Criminal Code) and France (the Gayssot Act of 1990) criminalise forms of speech such as Holocaust denial, and Britain restricts incitement to hatred. No state has unlimited speech.
Arabic

وَلَا تَسُبُّوا الَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ مِن دُونِ اللَّهِ فَيَسُبُّوا اللَّهَ عَدْوًا بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ

6:108
Objections

“So Muslims want to kill anyone who draws the Prophet.”

No. The classical law gives the penalty to a court, the Quran commands leaving mocking gatherings (4:140), and the Prophet’s own example was patience (Bukhari 6024, 3231). Individuals who kill over insult act against Islamic law, and many scholars have said so after each such incident.

“Free speech is an absolute value in the West.”

It is not. Germany and France criminalise Holocaust denial, the UK criminalises incitement to hatred, and until 2008 England had a blasphemy offence, and Ireland abolished its own blasphemy offence by referendum in 2018 and statute in 2019. The real question is which speech a society chooses to restrict and why. Muslims say the sacred also deserves protection, and you may disagree, but then we are discussing where to draw a line, not whether to draw one.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Islam punishes blasphemy by death. That is the opposite of free speech.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

The classical schools do treat insulting the Prophet ﷺ as grave, and they differ on the penalty and on non-Muslims. But the Quran tells believers to leave mocking gatherings (4:140) and repel evil with good (41:34), and the Prophet ﷺ answered abuse with patience (Bukhari 6024). Private violence is forbidden, and modern laws like Pakistan’s 295-C go beyond the classical rulings and are abused. And no society has absolute free speech: Germany and France criminalise Holocaust denial, and England had a blasphemy law until 2008.

In one breath

Classical schools treat insulting the Prophet ﷺ as grave but differ on penalties, the Quran and Prophet teach patience, vigilantism is forbidden, modern statutes overreach, and no society has unlimited speech.
