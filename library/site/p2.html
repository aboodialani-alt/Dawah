Jesus is not God in the New Testament

10 arguments

The question is not whether later Christians believed Jesus was God; they did. The question is whether the New Testament, and above all Jesus himself, teaches it. The method in this section has two halves. First, the positive case: Jesus' own words consistently distinguish him from God, and the attributes he displays are human, not divine. Second, the defensive case: the verses used as "proof texts" for his deity are either textually uncertain, mistranslated, or say less than claimed. The Quran's summary is the conclusion this section builds toward: "The Messiah, son of Mary, was no more than a messenger; messengers passed away before him… they both used to eat food" (5:75).

Argument 1 of 10

Those who knew Jesus called him a man and a prophet

Thesis

The people closest to Jesus, including his own disciples after the resurrection, described him as a man and a prophet sent by God, not as God.

Argument
  1. P1If Jesus had taught that he was God, his disciples and the earliest preachers would describe him that way.
  2. P2Instead, the Gospels and Acts repeatedly describe him as "a prophet" and "a man approved by God", including in the first public sermon of the church.
  3. ∴The earliest witnesses did not understand Jesus to be God.
Evidence
  • The crowds: "This is the prophet Jesus, from Nazareth of Galilee" (Matt 21:11); "a great prophet has arisen among us; God has visited His people" (Luke 7:16).
  • The disciples on the road to Emmaus, after the crucifixion, describe him as "a prophet mighty in deed and word before God and all the people" (Luke 24:19).
  • Jesus about himself: "you seek to kill me, a man who has told you the truth that I heard from God" (John 8:40).
  • Peter's first sermon at Pentecost, the founding proclamation of the church: "Jesus of Nazareth, a man attested to you by God with mighty works and wonders and signs that God did through him" (Acts 2:22). Notice who does the miracles: God, through him.
  • Others in John call him a prophet: the Samaritan woman (4:19), the crowd after the feeding (6:14, "truly the Prophet who is to come into the world"), the crowd at the feast (7:40), the blind man (9:17).

Many scholars (Dunn among them) hold that the Synoptic Gospels never explicitly call Jesus "God" and that there is no clear sign Jesus thought of himself as divine, though others dispute this. James Dunn, a believing Christian scholar, adds that the Christian worship of Jesus did not take the form of cultic worship (latreia) given to God.

Acts 2 is after the resurrection and after the Spirit was given, at the moment the apostles are said to be filled with understanding. If they still described him as a man attested by God, that is the apostolic understanding.

Argument 2 of 10

Jesus distinguishes himself from the one true God

Thesis

In his own words, Jesus places himself under God, calls the Father "the only true God", and calls God "my God".

Argument
  1. P1If Jesus were God, he would not deny goodness to himself, call someone else "the only true God", or say that he has a God.
  2. P2Jesus does all three (Mark 10:18; John 17:3; John 20:17), and says "the Father is greater than I" (John 14:28).
  3. ∴Jesus did not present himself as God.
Evidence

"Why do you call me good?" When a man addressed him as "Good Teacher", Jesus answered: "Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone" (Mark 10:18; Luke 18:19). This was so uncomfortable that Matthew, copying Mark, rewrote it: "Why do you ask me about what is good? There is only one who is good" (Matt 19:17). Justin (Dialogue 101) and the Clementine Homilies quote a longer form, "One is good, my Father in heaven"; Irenaeus and Hippolytus report Gnostic groups using it; Origen (CC 5.11) has "none is good but one, God the Father", as W.L. Petersen documents. The point is not that Jesus was morally bad; Barnabas is called "a good man" (Acts 11:24). It is that Jesus reserves God's absolute goodness for God alone and refuses to share it.

"The only true God". In his prayer Jesus says: "This is eternal life, that they know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent" (John 17:3). The Father is "the only true God"; Jesus is the one sent. This sentence is so clear that Augustine had to rearrange it to read "that they may know You and Jesus Christ whom You have sent as the only true God" (Tractates on John 105). The same structure appears in 1 Corinthians 8:6: "for us there is one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ".

"My God and your God". After the resurrection Jesus says to Mary Magdalene: "I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God" (John 20:17). In Revelation, the risen Jesus says "my God" four times in one verse (Rev 3:12). God has no God.

Dependence. "The Son can do nothing of himself, but only what he sees the Father doing" (John 5:19); "I can do nothing on my own… I seek not my own will but the will of Him who sent me" (5:30); "the Father is greater than I" (14:28); "my teaching is not mine but His who sent me" (7:16).

Argument 3 of 10

Jesus lacked the attributes of God

Thesis

God is all-knowing, cannot be tempted, does not grow, and cannot die. The Gospels show Jesus did not know the Hour, grew in wisdom, was tempted, and died.

Argument
  1. P1Whoever lacks an essential attribute of God is not God.
  2. P2Jesus lacked omniscience (Mark 13:32), immutability (Luke 2:52), the impossibility of being tempted (James 1:13 vs Hebrews 4:15), and immortality (1 Timothy 6:16 vs his death).
  3. ∴Jesus is not God.
Evidence

Ignorance of the Hour. "Concerning that day or that hour, no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father" (Mark 13:32). The verse troubled the church so much that scribes deleted "nor the Son" from the parallel in Matthew 24:36 in many manuscripts, as Metzger and Ehrman document, and Luke leaves the saying out. Ambrose claimed the phrase was an Arian interpolation; Jerome noted it was missing in some Greek copies. Later the church condemned the "Agnoetae", who taught that Christ was ignorant of some things, even though earlier fathers such as Athanasius, Gregory of Nazianzus and Cyril had attributed ignorance to his humanity.

Some apologists say oiden ("knows") here means "declares". No Greek lexicon gives that meaning; the context is a parable about watchfulness because no one knows; and if "know" meant "declare", the angels would be forbidden to declare it, yet in Revelation 14:6-7 an angel announces "the hour of His judgement has come". Other passages show the same limit: he went to a fig tree "to see if he could find anything on it" and found nothing because "it was not the season for figs" (Mark 11:13).

Growth. "Jesus increased in wisdom and in stature" (Luke 2:52). God does not increase in wisdom.

Temptation. "God cannot be tempted with evil" (James 1:13); Jesus "was tempted in every way as we are" (Hebrews 4:15), and was tempted by Satan for forty days (Matt 4).

Death. God "alone has immortality" (1 Timothy 6:16) and is "the King eternal, immortal, invisible, the only God" (1 Tim 1:17). Jesus died, and "Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father" (Rom 6:4); God raised him (Acts 2:24, 32). The one who dies is not the immortal one, and the one who is raised is not the one who raises.

Prayer and need. Jesus prayed all night (Luke 6:12), asked for the cup to pass from him and submitted: "not my will but Yours" (Luke 22:42), and, in a passage some early manuscripts omit, an angel is said to have strengthened him (Luke 22:43).

Argument 4 of 10

The main proof texts are textually insecure

Thesis

Several of the clearest "Jesus is God" verses are later scribal changes or depend on disputed punctuation, which shows that later Christians felt the original text was not clear enough.

Argument
  1. P1A doctrine as central as Jesus' deity should rest on texts whose wording is secure.
  2. P2Several of the strongest proof texts (1 John 5:7, 1 Timothy 3:16, Acts 20:28, Romans 9:5) are either later additions, variants, or matters of punctuation added by editors, and the direction of change is often toward higher Christology.
  3. ∴The proof texts cannot bear the weight placed on them, and scribal changes show the doctrine shaped the text, not the other way round.
Evidence
  • 1 John 5:7 (the "Johannine Comma"). "There are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word and the Holy Ghost, and these three are one" (KJV). It is absent from every Greek manuscript before the 14th-15th centuries and from all early versions except the Latin. Modern translations remove it. It is the only verse that states the Trinity in so many words, and it is a late Latin interpolation.
  • 1 Timothy 3:16. The KJV reads "God was manifest in the flesh". The earliest manuscripts read "who was manifested". In Greek capitals the difference is a stroke: ΟΣ ("who") became ΘΣ, the abbreviation for "God". Metzger says theos arose either through misreading OS as ΘΣ or deliberately, mainly to supply a subject for the six verbs and, less probably, for greater dogmatic precision (Textual Commentary, 2nd ed., p. 574).
  • Acts 20:28. "The church of God, which he obtained with his own blood". Several important manuscripts read "the church of the Lord". Even with "God", the Greek dia tou haimatos tou idiou can also be read "with the blood of His own [one]", that is His own Son, just as Romans 8:32 says God "did not spare His own Son".
  • Rom 9:5 is read both ways: most commentators take "God over all" of Christ, while the RSV, NEB footnote and others punctuate it as a separate doxology to the Father. The RSV reads: "…the Christ. God who is over all be blessed for ever." Paul never elsewhere calls Jesus "God over all"; for him "God" is the Father (1 Cor 8:6).
  • John 1:18. Manuscripts divide between "the only-begotten Son" and "the only-begotten God"; most critical editions prefer "God" on the evidence of P66, P75, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus.
Objections

"The Trinity doesn't depend on the Comma; scholars agree it's spurious."

Good, then the question becomes why it was added, and why the KJV tradition defended it for centuries. It shows that the church felt the need for an explicit text that the authentic NT does not provide.

"There are still enough verses."

Then the case should be made from texts like John 17:3 and Mark 10:18, which are secure and say the opposite. The fact that the direction of corruption always runs toward a "higher" Jesus is evidence about how the doctrine developed.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Textual variants don't change any doctrine. Every major doctrine is supported by secure texts.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

The examples show the opposite pressure: scribes changed "who" to "God", added "these three are one", and deleted "nor the Son". Even Ehrman's The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture documents changes made for theological reasons. A text being altered to support a doctrine suggests the doctrine was not secure in the original.

In one breath

The Johannine Comma is a late forgery, "God" in 1 Timothy 3:16 was originally "who", Acts 20:28 has variants and means "the blood of His own", and Romans 9:5 depends on modern punctuation. Each change moved toward a higher Christology.

Argument 5 of 10

John 1:1: "the Word was God" does not mean Jesus is the one God

Thesis

John 1:1 distinguishes "the God" (ho theos) from the Word, who is "god" (theos) without the article. Early Christians, including Origen, read it as making the Word divine but subordinate, which is heresy by later standards.

Argument
  1. P1If John 1:1 taught that the Word is the one God, the earliest interpreters would have read it that way.
  2. P2The text says the Word was "with the God" (pros ton theon) and then "the Word was god" (theos, no article). Origen and some second-century apologists (Justin, Tatian, Theophilus) read it as making the Word a derived, subordinate divinity.
  3. ∴John 1:1 does not teach that the Word is the one true God; at most it teaches a subordinate divine being, which neither Islam nor Nicene Christianity accepts.
Evidence

The Greek. "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God (ton theon, with the article), and the Word was God (theos, without the article)." Origen, the greatest Christian scholar of the 3rd century, explained the difference explicitly: John uses the article for the uncreated God, and leaves it off for the Word because the Word is "not God simply" but "god" in a derived sense, though more exalted than other "gods" (Commentary on John 2.2).

The Logos background. Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish contemporary of Jesus, called the Logos "the second God" (Questions on Genesis 2.62), a divine intermediary below God, not YHWH. In the Hebrew Bible, God's "word" is His speech: "God said, let there be light" (Gen 1:3); "by the word of the LORD the heavens were made" (Ps 33:6). Proverbs 8 personifies Wisdom as a "she" who was with God and helped create, yet no one thinks Wisdom is a person of the godhead. John personifies God's creative word the same way, and that word became flesh in the sense that God's word was embodied in Jesus' life and message.

Second-century readings. Justin, Tatian, Theophilus, Hippolytus, Tertullian and Novatian are usually read as holding a "two-stage" Logos theology: the Word was internal to God, then at a point in time God "begot" or put forth the Word for creation. Theophilus says God "begot this Word… the first-born of all creation" (To Autolycus 2.22). This makes the Son's existence as a distinct being begin, which Nicaea later condemned. So the earliest readers of John 1:1 did not read it as Nicene Christians do.

"All things were made through him." This describes agency, not uncreated status. Eve is called "the mother of all living" (Gen 3:20) without being the source of life.

Objections

"Colwell's rule: a definite predicate noun before the verb can drop the article and still be definite."

Colwell's rule says definite predicates often lack the article, not that anarthrous predicates are definite. Most grammarians now read theos here as qualitative ("divine"), not "the God". Origen, a native Greek speaker, read it as lesser.

"Jehovah's Witnesses say 'a god', and that's polytheism."

We do not need "a god". The point is that the verse distinguishes the Word from "the God", and the Word is God's creative speech personified. Islam affirms that Jesus is "a word from Him" (3:45; 4:171) because he was created by God's command "Be".

The "Trinity in the Old Testament" refuted

8 arguments

Christian apologists often argue that the Trinity is "hidden" in the Old Testament: in the plural Elohim, in "Let us make man", in the Angel of the LORD, in Psalm 110 and in Isaiah 9:6. The problem is that the people who wrote and read these texts, the Jews, never found a Trinity in them, and Christian scholars mostly agree. Augustine and the Catholic Encyclopedia both concede that the doctrine was not revealed in the Old Testament. This section first sets out the positive case that the Hebrew Bible teaches one God who is one person, then answers each proof text in turn.

Argument 1 of 8

The Hebrew Bible teaches one God who is a single person

Thesis

The Old Testament speaks of God with singular pronouns and verbs thousands of times, says He created "alone, by Myself", and was never read as triune by the people it was given to.

Argument
  1. P1If God were three persons, the revelation given to Israel would say so, or at least Israel would have understood it.
  2. P2The Old Testament insists God is one and alone, uses singular grammar for Him, and Rabbinic Judaism has not read it as teaching three persons. Christian scholars admit the doctrine is not revealed there.
  3. ∴The Old Testament teaches unitarian monotheism, the same monotheism as the Quran.
Evidence
  • The Shema. "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one (echad)" (Deut 6:4). Echad is the ordinary Hebrew numeral "one": "God took one of his ribs" (Gen 2:21). The scholar S.R. Driver explains that the verse contrasts the one LORD with the many local Baals; it is about number, not a "compound unity".
  • Alone. "I am the LORD, who made all things, who alone stretched out the heavens, who spread out the earth by Myself" (Isa 44:24). "I am the LORD and there is no other; besides Me there is no God" (Isa 45:5). "Have we not all one Father? Has not one God created us?" (Mal 2:10).
  • Jesus' own Shema. When asked which commandment is first, Jesus quoted Deuteronomy 6:4 and the scribe replied, "You are right, Teacher, He is one and there is no other besides Him", and Jesus said he was "not far from the kingdom of God" (Mark 12:29-34).
  • Christian concessions. The Catholic Encyclopedia says the doctrine of the Trinity is not clearly revealed in the Old Testament, though Augustine and other Fathers read hints of it there. Rabbinic Judaism has consistently rejected it.

Argument 2 of 8

Thesis

Argument 3 of 8

Thesis

Argument
  1. P1In the Bible and the ancient Near East, an agent speaks in the first person for his principal; prophets and royal messengers do it routinely.
  2. P2The Angel of the LORD is called a messenger (mal'akh), is sent, and speaks God's words. The Bible says God spoke "mouth to mouth" with Moses, unlike other prophets (Num 12:6-8), though Jacob says he saw God "face to face" (Gen 32:30). The Angel appears visibly to many.
  3. ∴The Angel of the LORD is a messenger speaking for God, not God Himself.
Evidence

First-person agency is normal. A prophet in Judges 6:8-10 says "Thus says the LORD… I brought you up from Egypt". David's messengers speak to Abigail in David's name, and she bows to them and calls herself David's servant (1 Sam 25:40-41). Abner's messengers speak in the first person for Abner (2 Sam 3:12). In Genesis 22:15-16 the angel says "By Myself I have sworn, declares the LORD", which is quoting God, not being Him.

God's appearance is limited. In Numbers 12:6-8 God says He reveals Himself to prophets only in visions and dreams, but to Moses "mouth to mouth". Manoah fears death "because we have seen God", but Pharaoh also told Moses that whoever saw his face again would die (Exod 10:28). Holy ground: there are holy nations, holy angels and holy lands.

"In Exodus 3 the angel says 'I am the God of Abraham'."

How the Trinity developed

4 arguments

If the Trinity were the teaching of Jesus, we would expect the earliest Christians to hold it and later Christians to repeat it. Thesis

Nearly every major Christian writer before Nicaea taught that the Son was subordinate to the Father, came into existence by the Father's will, or was in some sense created. By the standard of 381 they were heretics.

Argument
  1. P1If the Trinity were the original apostolic faith, the fathers closest to the apostles would teach it.
  2. P2The 2nd- and 3rd-century fathers taught that the Father alone is uncreated, that the Son was begotten by an act of the Father's will at a point in time, and that the Son is lower than the Father.
  3. ∴The Trinity was not the faith of the early church; it developed later.
Evidence
  • 1 Clement 59:4 (c. 96), among the earliest Christian writings outside the NT: "that all nations may know that You alone are God, and that Jesus Christ is Your servant/child (pais)".
  • Ignatius (c. 110): Jesus is "Son of God according to the will and power of God" (Smyrnaeans 1). The later "long recension" of his letters inflates his Christology. Newman wrote that, judged only by their express statements, "Ignatius may be considered a Patripassian, Justin arianizes, and Hippolytus is a Photinian", and used this to argue the Fathers' words were incomplete, not heretical.
  • The Shepherd of Hermas (2nd century): the Son is exalted for his obedience, and the Holy Spirit is identified with the Son.
  • Justin Martyr (c. 150): the Logos is "another God and Lord subject to the Maker of all things" (Dialogue 56), begotten "by an act of the Father's will" (Dialogue 61, 128), and the Father cannot appear on earth while the Son can (Dialogue 127). The Catholic Encyclopedia speaks of his "unfortunate concepts of the temporal and voluntary generation of the Word, and subordinationism".
  • Irenaeus (c. 180) speaks of the Son receiving dominion from the Father, but also says the Word was always with the Father (AH 2.30.9; 4.20.3), and the Father excels the Son in knowledge (2.28).
  • Theophilus of Antioch, the first to use the word trias ("triad": God, His Word, His Wisdom), says God "begot" the Word before creation (To Autolycus 2.10, 2.22).
  • Tertullian (c. 200), the first to use trinitas and persona: "there was a time when the Son was not" (Against Hermogenes 3); "the Father is the entire substance, but the Son is a derivation and portion of the whole" (Against Praxeas 9).   • Origen (c. 230) taught the Son is subordinate to the Father but eternally begotten (De Principiis 1.2.9; Commentary on John 2.6). The Father's power is greater than the Son's, and the Son's than the Spirit's; and "we should not pray to any begotten being, not even to Christ himself, but only to the Father" (On Prayer 15). He was anathematised in 553.
  • Novatian: "the Father alone has no beginning" (On the Trinity 31).
  • Dionysius of Alexandria (c. 260) wrote in a letter that the Son is "a creature and made", language Athanasius says he later clarified (On the Opinion of Dionysius 4-18).   • Eusebius of Caesarea, the church historian who attended Nicaea: the Son is "not eternally coexistent with the Father" and is "Lord in the second degree" (Demonstration of the Gospel 5.1; 4.15). Jerome called him the "most open defender of the heresy of Arius".

"They were working out the language; their faith was Trinitarian."

Justin, Tertullian and Origen did not merely lack the words; they explicitly affirmed what Nicaea condemned: the Son begotten by will, a time when the Son was not, the Father alone uncreated. That is a different belief, not imprecise wording for the same one.

Argument 4 of 4

The doctrine is logically incoherent

Thesis

The Trinity claims that the Father is God, the Son is God, the Spirit is God, the three are distinct, and there is only one God. These claims cannot all be true together under the ordinary meaning of "is".

Argument

  2. P2The Trinity denies that the Father is the Son.
  3. P3If "is God" means only "is divine", then there are three divine individuals, which is three gods unless "God" names a shared nature rather than a person.
  4. P4But the God of the Bible is a person ("I", "He") who speaks, acts and loves, not an abstract nature.
  5. ∴Every way of reading the Trinity either collapses into one person (modalism), three gods (tritheism), or an impersonal "God", so the doctrine is incoherent.
Evidence

The classical statement of the problem is the "logical problem of the Trinity". Christian philosophers have proposed solutions, such as "relative identity" (the Father is the same God as the Son but not the same person). Critics point out that relative identity abandons the normal logic of identity, and that God has properties that should transfer under identity: if the Father begot and the Son did not, they differ in a property, so they cannot be identical to the same thing.

The incarnation adds a second problem. God is immutable: "I the LORD do not change" (Mal 3:6). If the Logos took on a human nature at a point in time, something about the Logos changed. If nothing changed, it is unclear what "became flesh" means.

Christian thinkers have often conceded the point. Luther admitted that the Trinity and two natures are beyond and contrary to reason, to be accepted on faith. The church calls the doctrine a "mystery". But a mystery is something we cannot fully understand; a contradiction is something that cannot be true. The Quran's challenge is simple: "Say, He is Allah, One" (112:1), and "had there been gods besides Allah in the heavens and earth, they would have been ruined" (21:22).

Argument 1 of 8

What the Quran actually claims

Thesis

The Quran denies that Jesus was killed or crucified, says the event was made to appear so, and says Allah raised him. This answers the Jewish boast and notes that the Christians themselves were "in doubt".

Argument
  1. P1To refute a claim, you must first state it accurately.
  2. P2Quran 4:157 is a reply to a specific boast, "we killed the Messiah", and adds that "those who differ over it are in doubt about it, with no knowledge except following assumption".
  3. ∴The Quran's claim is precise: Jesus was not killed or crucified; it appeared so to observers; disputes about it rest on conjecture.
Evidence

The passage (4:153-158) is a list of the crimes of a group of the Children of Israel: breaking the covenant, killing prophets, slandering Mary with "a great calumny" (4:156), and saying "we killed the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, the messenger of Allah". The Babylonian Talmud has a late tradition that "on the eve of Passover they hanged Yeshu" (Sanhedrin 43a), though scholars debate its date and reliability, and slanders about Mary appear in the same literature (Shabbat 104b; Sanhedrin 67a).

Shubbiha lahum means "it was made to look like that to them". Commentators differ on how: one view is that another man was given his likeness (the substitution view), another that the scene was confused and people thought he had died. Both fit the text. The phrase "those who differ over it are in doubt" fits the Christians, who in the first centuries disagreed sharply about what happened at the cross (see below).

Argument 2 of 8

The earliest Jesus sources say nothing of the crucifixion or resurrection

Thesis

The oldest reconstructable layers of the Jesus tradition (the Q sayings source, the Gospel of Thomas, the Epistle of James) have no passion narrative and no resurrection, and the passion predictions in the Gospels look like later additions.

Argument
  1. P1If the crucifixion and resurrection were the centre of Jesus' own message, the earliest collections of his teaching would contain them.
  2. P2Q, Thomas and James contain no account of his death or resurrection; the disciples in the Gospels behave as if Jesus never predicted them; and Mark, the earliest Gospel, originally ended with no resurrection appearance.
  3. ∴The crucifixion-resurrection message was not part of the earliest teaching tradition; it was built up later.
Evidence
  • Q. The sayings source shared by Matthew and Luke has no passion narrative and no resurrection, though it mentions bearing a cross (Q 14:27) and the killing of prophets.
  • The Gospel of Thomas, 114 sayings of Jesus, has no account of the crucifixion and no resurrection appearances.
  • The Epistle of James, traditionally attributed to Jesus' brother (a point many scholars dispute), never mentions the cross, the resurrection or atonement.
  • Mark's ending. The earliest manuscripts of Mark (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) end at 16:8: the women flee from the empty tomb "and they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid". There is no appearance of the risen Jesus. Verses 9-20 were added later.
  • Passion predictions. Jesus predicts his death and rising on the third day again and again (Mark 8:31, 9:31, 10:33-34). Yet at his arrest the disciples flee, no one goes to the tomb expecting a resurrection, the women bring spices to anoint a corpse, and the disciples dismiss the women's report as "nonsense" (Luke 24:11). That behaviour makes sense only if the predictions were not made. Bultmann and some others regard the detailed predictions as written after the fact; many scholars accept that Jesus expected a violent death. The Oxford Bible Commentary on Mark says they "owe a lot to later Christian creativity".
  • No atonement in Luke-Acts. Bart Ehrman argues that Luke omits Mark's "ransom for many" and that Acts mostly presents the death as a miscarriage of justice, although Acts 8:32-35 and 20:28 are read by others as atonement language.
Objections

"Q is a sayings collection; you wouldn't expect a narrative."

That is the point: the earliest followers preserved Jesus' teaching, and his teaching did not centre on a coming atoning death. If the cross were the core of the message, it would appear among the sayings.

"Mark 16:8 implies the resurrection through the empty tomb."

An empty tomb is exactly what the Quran's account produces: Jesus was raised. It is not evidence of a death followed by resurrection.

Practice

In one breath

Q, Thomas and James have no passion or resurrection; Mark originally ended without any appearance; and the disciples act as if Jesus never predicted his death. The cross-centred message developed later.

Argument 3 of 8

Early followers of Jesus disputed the crucifixion

Thesis

From the time of Paul onward, some Christians denied that Jesus died on the cross or said someone else was crucified, which is the "doubt" the Quran mentions.

Argument
  1. P1If the crucifixion were universally accepted by Jesus' early followers, we would find no early Christians denying it.
  2. P2Paul, Ignatius, Polycarp and Irenaeus all argue against Christians who denied or reinterpreted the crucifixion, and several early groups said another man died in Jesus' place.
  3. ∴The crucifixion was disputed among Jesus' followers from the earliest period, as Quran 4:157 says.
Evidence
  • Paul's opponents. Paul calls a crucified Christ "a stumbling block to Jews" (1 Cor 1:23), though that verse does not say anyone denied the crucifixion. He says believers turn away from his message of Christ crucified (Gal 3:1), and speaks of "enemies of the cross of Christ" (Phil 3:18). His opponents preached "another Jesus" (2 Cor 11:4).
  • Ignatius (d. c. 110) refers to Christ's death, "which some deny" (Magnesians 9), and to people who said he "only seemed to suffer" (Trallians 10; Smyrnaeans 2). In Philadelphians 8 he records an argument with people who said: "If I don't find it in the archives (the original documents), I don't believe it in the gospel." When Ignatius replied "it is written", they answered "that is the question". Scholars like Myllykoski argue these opponents were Jewish Christians, not Greek dualists.
  • Polycarp: "whoever does not confess the testimony of the cross is of the devil" (Philippians 7.1), which only makes sense if some did not.
  • Basilides (early 2nd century), as reported by Irenaeus (Against Heresies 1.24.4): Simon of Cyrene was crucified in Jesus' likeness while Jesus stood by. Hippolytus' account differs.
  • The Second Treatise of the Great Seth (Nag Hammadi): "It was another… Simon, who bore the cross on his shoulder… I was laughing."
  • The Mandaeans, a later non-Christian group that reveres John the Baptist, reject Jesus as Messiah and some of their texts deny his crucifixion.
  • Dale Allison notes some early believers may have held that "Jesus only seemed to die" and ascended like Enoch and Elijah (Constructing Jesus, p. 57). He also notes that Mark 15:44-45, where Pilate checks that Jesus is dead, has no parallel in Matthew or Luke and may have been added "to counter the polemic that Jesus had not died".
Objections

"These were Gnostics who thought matter was evil; nothing like Islam."

Basilides and the Seth treatise were Gnostic, but in Philadelphians 8 Ignatius' opponents appealed to "the archives" (the Jewish scriptures), though that dispute is about scriptural authority, not the crucifixion, which is a Jewish-Christian argument. The Quran agrees with none of the Gnostic metaphysics; it agrees only that the crucifixion was a matter of dispute and appearance.

"These groups are late and marginal."

Paul's opponents are mid-1st century and Ignatius' early 2nd century; the Seth treatise is 3rd century.

Historians accept that Jesus' followers believed he was crucified, and the Quran says it was made to appear so. But "every historian" is not the same as "every early Christian": the records of Paul, Ignatius and Polycarp show Christians who denied or doubted it within living memory.

Argument 4 of 8

The "independent" non-Christian witnesses are late, dependent or doctored

Thesis

The non-Christian sources cited for the crucifixion (Josephus, Tacitus, Thallus, Mara bar Serapion) are either interpolated, report what Christians believed decades later, or do not mention Jesus at all.

Argument
  1. P1An independent witness must be early, authentic and not dependent on Christian reports.
  2. P2Josephus' passage on Jesus has been rewritten by Christians; Tacitus wrote 80 years later about what Christians believed; Thallus is lost and never names Jesus; Mara bar Serapion never names Jesus or a crucifixion.
  3. ∴There is no independent, contemporary non-Christian confirmation of the crucifixion; all we have is evidence of what Christians believed, which the Quran grants.
Evidence

Josephus (the "Testimonium Flavianum", Antiquities 18.3.3, c. 93). As it stands it calls Jesus "the Christ" and says he rose on the third day, which no believing Jew would write. No Christian writer cites it before Eusebius (c. 324), although at least eight earlier fathers quote Josephus. Origen, who knew Josephus well, says twice that Josephus did not believe Jesus was the Christ (Contra Celsum 1.47; Commentary on Matthew 10.17), which contradicts the text we have. Louis Feldman's survey of 87 discussions (1937-1980) found only 13 treating the passage as wholly interpolated; most accepted a partly authentic core. Some scholars (K.A. Olson and others) argue Eusebius composed it. Eusebius titled Praeparatio Evangelica 12.31 "that it will be necessary sometimes to use falsehood as a remedy", in a chapter citing Plato on the "noble lie", and the historian Jacob Burckhardt called him "the first thoroughly dishonest historian of antiquity".

Many scholars do hold that a shorter core of the passage is authentic. Even so, a core saying Pilate condemned Jesus to the cross, written sixty years later in Rome, tells us what was reported, not what happened, and the Quran already grants that it appeared so.

Tacitus (Annals 15.44, c. 116). He says Christus "suffered the extreme penalty under Pontius Pilate". He writes over 80 years later, while explaining who the Christians were, so he is reporting Christian belief or common knowledge about Christians. Bart Ehrman says Tacitus' information "is not particularly helpful" for establishing the historical Jesus.

Others. Thallus' work is lost; we know it through Julius Africanus as quoted by George Syncellus (c. 800), and Thallus apparently only mentioned an eclipse, not Jesus. Mara bar Serapion mentions an unnamed "wise king" of the Jews, says nothing of a crucifixion, and survives in a 7th-century manuscript. Pliny and Suetonius describe Christians, not the event.

Objections

"Even sceptics like Ehrman accept the crucifixion."

Ehrman accepts the crucifixion on several grounds, including Paul, multiple Gospels, Josephus and Tacitus, and the embarrassment of a crucified Messiah (a crucified Messiah was embarrassing, so Christians wouldn't invent it). But the crucifixion became the centre of Paul's theology, which shows it was useful, not embarrassing. And scholars such as Rafael Rodríguez and Dale Allison have shown that these criteria are subjective. If all four Gospels proudly report it, it was not something Christians were embarrassed to tell.

"Multiple attestation in the Gospels proves it."

The Gospels depend on each other (Matthew and Luke copy Mark), so they are not independent witnesses, and all reflect the community's belief, which the Quran grants.

Argument 5 of 8

The Gospel accounts of the death and resurrection contradict each other

Thesis

On the day, the hour, who carried the cross, what Jesus said, who went to the tomb, what they saw, and where Jesus appeared, the Gospels disagree.

Argument
  1. P1Eyewitness-based accounts of the central event of a faith should agree on its basic facts.
  2. P2The four Gospels contradict each other on many basic facts of the crucifixion and resurrection.
  3. ∴The accounts are not reliable records of what happened; they are developing traditions, which fits "they are in doubt about it" (4:157).
Evidence
  • The day. In Mark, Jesus eats the Passover meal and is crucified the next day (Mark 14:12; 15:25). In John, he is crucified on "the day of Preparation of the Passover", before the meal (John 19:14).
  • The hour. Mark: "it was the third hour when they crucified him" (15:25). John: at "about the sixth hour" Jesus was still before Pilate (19:14).
  • The cross. Simon of Cyrene carried it (Mark 15:21, Matthew, Luke); Jesus carried it himself (John 19:17). Some scholars think John is countering the tradition that Simon was crucified in his place.
  • The robbers. Both mocked him (Mark 15:32); one rebuked the other and was promised paradise (Luke 23:39-43).
  • The wine. Jesus vowed not to drink wine until the kingdom came (Matt 26:29), but in John he drinks sour wine on the cross (19:29-30).
  • Last words. "My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34); "Father, into Your hands I commit my spirit" (Luke 23:46); "It is finished" (John 19:30).
  • At the tomb. One young man (Mark), one angel (Matthew), two men (Luke), two angels (John). The women told no one (Mark 16:8) or told everything to the eleven (Luke 24:9).
  • Where he appeared. In Matthew the disciples are sent to Galilee (28:10, 16). In Luke they are told to stay in Jerusalem (24:49).
  • How long. Luke's Gospel seems to have Jesus ascend on the day of the resurrection (24:50-51), while Acts, by the same author, says forty days (Acts 1:3).
  • Who saw him first. Mary Magdalene (John 20); the two Marys (Matthew 28); Peter, according to Paul's list (1 Cor 15:5), which also says "the Twelve" though Judas was dead and Matthew says "the eleven".
  • Three days and three nights. Jesus said he would be in the earth "three days and three nights" (Matt 12:40), but Friday afternoon to Sunday dawn is at most two nights.
Objections

"Different perspectives aren't contradictions. Witnesses always vary in details."

Some can be harmonised with effort, but not all: Jesus cannot be crucified both before and after the Passover meal, both carry and not carry the cross, and the disciples cannot both go to Galilee and stay in Jerusalem. C.K. Barrett said there is no point trying to harmonise the wine passages.

"The core agrees: he died and rose."

The core is what was believed. The Quran agrees that people believed it. The question is whether these accounts are reliable enough to overturn revelation, and their disagreement on basic facts shows they are not.

Argument 7 of 8

The Shroud, the swoon theory and the Nazareth Inscription

Thesis

The physical "evidence" Christians cite for the death and resurrection fails: the Shroud of Turin is a medieval artefact and the Nazareth Inscription is not about Jesus. And when Christians cite sceptical historians, they ignore that the same historians reject the resurrection.

Argument
  1. P1Physical evidence must be authentic and relevant.
  2. P2The Shroud is dated to 1260-1390 and its first record calls it a painting; the Nazareth Inscription's marble comes from Kos and it never mentions Jesus.
  3. ∴Neither provides evidence for Jesus' death or resurrection.
Evidence

The Shroud of Turin. Its first documented exhibition was at Lirey in the 1350s; the 1389 memo from Bishop Pierre d'Arcis is the first record claiming it was painted. The memo was addressed to Pope Clement VII and said an earlier bishop had found it "cunningly painted" and that the artist had confessed. In 1988 three laboratories (Arizona, Oxford, Zürich) radiocarbon-dated it to AD 1260-1390. Walter McCrone found iron-oxide and vermilion pigment. Borrini and Garlaschelli (Journal of Forensic Sciences, 2018) found the bloodstain patterns inconsistent with a real body and concluded it was an "artistic or didactic representation" of the 14th century. Andrea Nicolotti has shown no Christian looked for Jesus' burial cloths before the 6th century, and many competing "shrouds" were venerated.

The Nazareth Inscription. A Roman decree against tomb robbing, sometimes linked to the empty tomb. It has no known provenance, never mentions Jesus, and a 2020 isotope study (Harper et al., Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports) traced the marble to Kos and proposed a link with the desecration of the tomb of the tyrant Nikias.

Survival. Some historians (Hugh Schonfield, Barbara Thiering, and others discussed by Allison and Vermes) have argued Jesus survived crucifixion. Josephus records that he saw three acquaintances crucified, had them taken down, and one survived (Life 420-421). Muslims do not need this theory, since the Quran says he was not crucified at all, but it shows that "he died" is not the only historical option.

Objections

"The radiocarbon sample came from a medieval repair."

This claim has been examined and rejected by the original laboratories; the sample area was chosen by textile experts, and later statistical reanalyses of the 1988 data have not changed the medieval date, though some found heterogeneity. The 1389 memo independently says it was painted.

Argument 8 of 8

"Mutawaffika" (3:55) does not mean Jesus died

The verb tawaffa means "to take fully", and the Quran uses it for sleep. This answers both Christians and Ahmadis who claim the Quran admits his death.

Argument
  1. P1A word's meaning is fixed by its use in the same text.
  2. P2The Quran uses tawaffa for both death (2:234; 16:70) and sleep (6:60; 39:42). Early commentators understood 3:55 as being taken alive.
  3. ∴3:55 says Allah took Jesus fully, body and soul, and raised him; it does not say he died.
Evidence
  • Sleep. "It is He who takes you (yatawaffakum) by night" (6:60); "Allah takes (yatawaffa) the souls at the time of their death, and those that do not die, in their sleep" (39:42). The Prophet's ﷺ waking supplication thanks Allah "who gave us life after He caused us to die", meaning sleep (Bukhari 6324).
  • Tabari reports the view that tawaffa here means taking him from the earth alive; al-Rabi' said he was raised while asleep; al-Hasan al-Basri reported in a mursal narration that the Prophet ﷺ told the Jews that "Jesus did not die; he will return to you before the Day of Resurrection".

The report that Ibn Abbas glossed mutawaffika as "I will cause you to die" comes through Ali ibn Abi Talha, who did not meet him, and Ibn Abbas himself placed that death after Jesus' return, since the conjunction "and" (wa) in Arabic does not impose sequence.

"Tawaffa usually means death."

Often, when the context is death. Here the context is Allah rescuing Jesus from those who plotted against him (3:54) and raising him (3:55), and 4:157-158 explicitly denies his killing and says Allah raised him. Tawaffa without death is attested in the Quran itself.

Jewish Christianity & the Ebionites

4 arguments

The first followers of Jesus were Jews in Jerusalem led by his brother James. They kept the Law, prayed at the Temple, and called Jesus the Messiah and a prophet. Over the next two centuries their descendants, known as Nazarenes and Ebionites, were pushed aside by a Gentile church shaped by Paul, and eventually labelled heretics. Their beliefs look strikingly like Islam's view of Jesus. This section argues three things: "Son of God" was a Jewish title for God's servants, not a claim to deity; the original Jesus community was closest to Islam; and Jesus himself practised what the Quran calls islam, submission to God.

Argument 1 of 4

"Son of God" and "Father" are titles of closeness, not deity

Thesis

In the Bible, "son of God" is used for Israel, kings, angels and righteous people. The early sermons in Acts call Jesus God's pais, "servant", which is exactly the Arabic 'Abdullah.

Argument
  1. P1If "son of God" is applied throughout the Bible to created beings, the title alone cannot prove deity.
  2. P2The Bible calls Israel, David, Adam, angels and peacemakers sons of God, and the earliest preaching in Acts calls Jesus God's servant.
  3. ∴"Son of God" means one close to and obedient to God; it is compatible with Jesus being a human prophet.

Paul

6 arguments

Most of what makes Christianity different from the religion of Jesus (the abolition of the Law, salvation by faith in the cross, the cosmic divine Christ) comes from Paul, a man who never met Jesus in his lifetime, persecuted his followers, and based his authority on a private vision. This section argues that Paul's authority was disputed by the original apostles, that he contradicted Jesus and the Torah on the Law, that his methods were openly adaptable, that his central prophecy failed, and that his Christology draws on Greek religious language. The point is not to attack Paul personally but to show why Christians cannot assume that Paul speaks for Jesus.

Argument 1 of 6

Paul's authority rests on a private vision and was disputed by the apostles

Thesis

Paul says he received his gospel from no human being, and the Jerusalem leaders (James and Peter) and their followers opposed him. Later Jewish-Christian texts (the Pseudo-Clementines, 3rd-4th century in their final form) have Peter denounce "the man who is my enemy", which many scholars read as an allusion to Paul.

Argument
  1. P1A claim to speak for Jesus is credible when it is confirmed by those Jesus himself taught.
  2. P2Paul says his gospel came by private revelation, not from the apostles (Gal 1:11-12), he publicly opposed Peter, he says rivals preached "another Jesus", and James' community demanded he prove he kept the Law (Acts 21).
  3. ∴Paul's version of the message was not the apostles' version and has no independent confirmation from Jesus' own disciples.
Evidence
  • Self-certified. "…the gospel that was preached by me is not man's gospel. For I did not receive it from any man, nor was I taught it, but I received it through a revelation of Jesus Christ" (Gal 1:11-12). He did not go to the apostles for three years after his conversion (Gal 1:17-18).
  • The vision itself is unstable. In Acts 9:7 his companions "heard the voice but saw no one"; in Acts 22:9 they "saw the light but did not hear the voice". In Acts 26 the commission comes directly from Jesus; in Acts 9 and 22 it comes later through Ananias.
  • Conflict with Peter and James. "When Cephas came to Antioch, I opposed him to his face" because "certain men came from James" and Peter withdrew from eating with Gentiles (Gal 2:11-12). Paul downplays the status of the Jerusalem leaders as "those who seemed to be influential (what they were makes no difference to me; God shows no partiality)" (Gal 2:6).
  • "Another Jesus". Paul says rival preachers came to Corinth with "another Jesus" and "a different gospel", and calls them "super-apostles" (2 Cor 11:4-5), and later "false apostles" (11:13). James Tabor argues these were envoys of James and Peter.
  • James' demand. When Paul reached Jerusalem, James told him thousands of believers were "all zealous for the Law" and had heard Paul taught Jews to abandon Moses; he advised Paul to join four men under a vow and pay their expenses, to show that Paul himself lived in observance of the Law (Acts 21:18-26).
  • "The enemy". In the Pseudo-Clementine writings, Peter complains of "the lawless and trifling preaching of the man who is my enemy", and warns believers to accept no teacher who does not bring a testimonial from James. Bishop Westcott acknowledged that "no doubt St Paul is referred to as 'the enemy'". The Ebionites rejected Paul as an apostate.
Objections

"Galatians 2:9 says James, Peter and John gave Paul the right hand of fellowship."

They agreed to a division of labour (Paul to the Gentiles, they to the Jews), yet the very next verses describe the Antioch clash. And Acts 21 shows James' community still suspicious years later.

"Peter calls Paul's letters scripture (2 Pet 3:16)."

2 Peter is widely regarded by scholars as pseudonymous, written decades after Peter's death, and it is among the books whose canonicity was disputed longest (see Corruption of the Bible). It even concedes that Paul's letters contain things "hard to understand".

Corruption, dating & authorship of the Bible

6 arguments

The Quran says the People of the Book changed their scripture: they "distort words from their places" (4:46), "write the book with their own hands" (2:79), and "conceal" parts of it (2:174). Christians often reply that the Bible is the best-attested book of antiquity. This section shows the opposite on the questions that matter for scripture: we do not know who wrote most of it, the Gospels are not eyewitness accounts, the church fathers themselves complained that texts were altered, major passages were added later, the Hebrew text contains deliberate theological changes, and the canon itself was disputed for centuries. Having many manuscripts tells you what later copies said; it does not tell you what was originally revealed.

Argument 1 of 6

The Gospels are anonymous and not eyewitness accounts

Thesis

None of the Gospels names its author; the names are first attested for all four Gospels around 180 CE; and most critical scholars hold that none is the work of an eyewitness.

Argument
  1. P1A text's authority as testimony depends on knowing who wrote it and whether they witnessed the events.
  2. P2The four Gospels are anonymous, their traditional names are first attested for all four around 180 (Irenaeus). Papias (c. 110-130, preserved only in Eusebius) speaks of a Mark who wrote down Peter's teaching “not in order” and a Matthew who compiled the sayings in the Hebrew dialect, but those descriptions do not fit our Mark and our Greek Matthew well, and whether he means them is disputed, so he does not settle the names. The Gospels were written 40-70 years after Jesus in Greek by authors who copy each other and make errors about Palestine.
  3. ∴The Gospels are not eyewitness testimony and cannot be treated as the Injil revealed to Jesus.
Evidence
  • Anonymous. No Gospel names its author in the text. The earliest Christian writers quote "the Gospel" without names (the Didache calls it "the Gospel of our Lord"). Irenaeus, around 180, is the first to name all four (Against Heresies 3.1.1). James Tabor: "All four Gospels are anonymous… names were assigned later by tradition."
  • Not eyewitnesses. The New Oxford Annotated Bible dates them roughly 40 to 70 years after the events. They are not eyewitness accounts. Richard Bauckham, who argues for eyewitness sources, acknowledges that his thesis challenges the form-critical consensus that the Gospel traditions circulated anonymously.
  • Matthew copies Mark. Papias said Matthew compiled "the sayings" (logia) in Hebrew, but our Greek Matthew reproduces most of Mark's content (roughly 80-90%) and about half of its exact wording. An apostle who saw the events would not need to copy a non-apostle's account.
  • Mark's geography. Mark has Jesus go from Tyre "through Sidon to the Sea of Galilee, through the region of the Decapolis" (Mark 7:31), a route like going from London to Paris via Edinburgh. This suggests an author unfamiliar with Palestine.
  • John. Ancient tradition gave at least four different authors. Papias distinguished John the apostle from "John the Elder", and Eusebius noted two tombs of "John" in Ephesus (Church History 3.39). The Alogi (Epiphanius, Panarion 51) attributed John's Gospel and Revelation to Cerinthus, and Gaius of Rome attributed Revelation to him (Eusebius 3.28.2). The final form is usually dated around 90-110.
  • Unreliable oral tradition. Irenaeus claimed, on the authority of "the elders" who knew John, that Jesus lived to nearly fifty (Against Heresies 2.22.5). If the elders' oral tradition could get that wrong, it is no guarantee for the Gospel names.
  • Other books. Origen says of Hebrews: "who wrote the epistle, in truth God knows" (Eusebius, Church History 6.25). Most scholars regard 2 Peter and the Pastoral letters (1-2 Timothy, Titus) as written in the names of apostles who did not write them. Acts is dated by some scholars (Pervo, Mack) into the 2nd century.

Argument 2 of 6

The church fathers themselves admitted the text was altered

Thesis

Celsus, Origen, Dionysius of Corinth and Jerome all complained, in the first four centuries, that Christian scriptures were being altered by scribes, sometimes deliberately.

Argument
  1. P1If those who copied and used the texts in the early centuries testify that they were being changed, the claim of perfect preservation fails.
  2. P2Origen, Jerome and others complain of deliberate additions and deletions, and Jerome says there were "almost as many forms of text as copies" of the Latin Gospels.
  3. ∴The New Testament text was not preserved unchanged; early Christians themselves say so.
Evidence
  • Celsus (c. 175), quoted by Origen: some believers "alter the original text of the gospel three or four or several times over, and change its character to enable them to deny difficulties" (Contra Celsum 2.27). Origen does not deny it; he says it was done by heretics.
  • Origen himself: "the differences among the manuscripts have become great, either through the negligence of some copyists or through the perverse audacity of others; they either neglect to check over what they have transcribed, or in the process of checking they make additions or deletions as they please" (Commentary on Matthew 15.14).
  • Dionysius of Corinth (c. 170) complained that his own letters had been altered and added: "no wonder some have attempted to adulterate the Lord's writings" (Eusebius, Church History 4.23).
  • Jerome (383), commissioned by Pope Damasus to revise the Latin Bible: there are "almost as many forms of text as there are copies", corrupted by inaccurate translators, "the blundering alterations of confident but ignorant critics", and copyists "more asleep than awake" (Preface to the Gospels). Bruce Metzger notes that Jerome catalogues the kinds of scribal error, including conscious emendation.
  • A scribe's own complaint. In Codex Vaticanus, at Hebrews 1:3, a later corrector changed a word, and another scribe wrote in the margin: "Fool and knave! Leave the old reading, don't change it!"
  • The Old Testament too. Justin Martyr accused the Jews of removing passages from Jeremiah and the Psalms (Dialogue 71-73), and quotes a "Jeremiah" text found in no manuscript. Chrysostom says Jews burned books and copies perished. Cardinal Bellarmine listed Justin, Origen, Eusebius and Chrysostom as holding that Jews had corrupted the Old Testament.
  • Helmut Koester: the text-types we can reconstruct go back only to around 200, and "substantial revisions" happened during the first hundred years.
Objections

"They're talking about heretics' copies, which were caught."

Origen speaks of "copyists" generally, and Jerome speaks of the copies in the church's own hands. If corruption was widespread enough for Jerome to see as many texts as copies, we cannot assume every change was caught, especially in the first century where we have almost no manuscripts.

"Textual criticism lets us recover the original."

It lets us recover the text as of the 2nd-3rd centuries, the earliest substantial manuscripts. Koester's point is that the most important changes would have happened before then.

Practice

Your opponent says

“You're quoting Christians who loved the Bible. They were complaining about scribes, not saying the Bible is false.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Exactly: they loved the Bible and still testified that copyists added and deleted "as they please". That is the Quran's charge (2:79; 4:46). Their love makes the testimony more credible, not less.

In one breath

Celsus, Origen, Dionysius of Corinth and Jerome all complained that texts were being altered; Jerome said there were almost as many forms of text as copies; a Vaticanus scribe wrote "Fool and knave, leave the old reading!"

Argument 3 of 6

Major passages were added later

Thesis

The longer ending of Mark, the story of the adulteress, the Trinitarian baptism formula, the Johannine Comma and several smaller changes are later additions or alterations, and many of them carry central doctrines.

Argument
  1. P1A passage absent from the earliest manuscripts and the earliest fathers, and inconsistent with its context, is a later addition.
  2. P2Mark 16:9-20, John 7:53-8:11 and 1 John 5:7 meet these tests clearly; Matthew 28:19 is textually secure in every Greek manuscript, but its formula is in tension with Acts and with Eusebius' shorter quotations.
  3. ∴Important passages of the New Testament, including texts on the resurrection appearances, forgiveness, and the Trinity, were added after the books were written.
Evidence

Mark 16:9-20. Absent from Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, the two oldest complete manuscripts. Eusebius and Jerome say almost all Greek copies of their time lacked it, and it is not in the Eusebian canon tables. Its style differs; it re-introduces Mary Magdalene as if she had not been mentioned (taking a detail from Luke 8:2); and it ignores Mark's own promise of a meeting in Galilee (14:28; 16:7). Scholars broadly agree it is not original to Mark and was added in the 2nd century or earlier (Irenaeus quotes it c. 180).

John 7:53-8:11 (the woman caught in adultery). Absent from P66, P75, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus and the early Syriac and Coptic versions; Origen and Chrysostom skip it in their commentaries; later manuscripts place it in different spots (even in Luke). Metzger: "the evidence for the non-Johannine origin of the pericope of the adulteress is overwhelming".

Matthew 28:19 ("baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit"). In Acts, the apostles baptise only "in the name of Jesus" (Acts 2:38; 8:16; 10:48; 19:5), which is strange if Jesus had commanded the triadic formula. Eusebius quotes the verse many times before Nicaea in a short form: "Go and make disciples of all nations in my name". The medieval (14th-century) Hebrew Matthew in Shem Tob's Even Bohan lacks the formula, though its antiquity is debated. Geza Vermes argues the formula is "unheard of anywhere in the NT" apart from Matthew 28:19 itself and the Didache (7.1), and is of late Gentile origin. It also conflicts with Jesus' instruction "go nowhere among the Gentiles" (Matt 10:5-6) and "I was sent only to the lost sheep of Israel" (15:24).

1 John 5:7. "The Father, the Word and the Holy Ghost, and these three are one" appears in no Greek manuscript before the late Middle Ages and was not quoted by any Greek father in the Trinitarian debates, where it would have been decisive. It entered the Greek printed text through Erasmus' third edition (1522).

Smaller theological changes.

  • Luke 23:32: the earliest manuscripts read "other criminals, two, were led away with him" (heteroi kakourgoi duo), which could imply Jesus was a criminal; later scribes reordered it to "two others, criminals".
  • John 7:8: Jesus says "I am not going up to this feast", then goes (7:10). Scribes changed it to "not yet" (already found in P66 and P75, earlier than Porphyry's criticism), while Epiphanius, Jerome and others knew the reading "not".
  • Matthew 24:36: "nor the Son" deleted (see Jesus is not God).
  • Mark 1:1: "the Son of God" is missing from the first hand of Sinaiticus.
Objections

"No doctrine depends on these passages."

The longer ending of Mark is the only resurrection appearance in Mark; the Comma was the only explicit Trinity verse; Matthew 28:19 is the only Trinitarian baptism command. That these are the passages under suspicion is exactly the point: the doctrines grew, and texts were added to match.

"Matthew 28:19 is in every Greek manuscript."

Our Greek manuscripts of Matthew 28 begin in the 4th century. Eusebius' pre-Nicene quotations and the practice in Acts are evidence of an earlier form. You can concede the textual question is debated while pointing out the tension with Acts and Matthew 10:5-6.

Practice

Your opponent says

“If the Bible was corrupted, why do Christians still have the original words in footnotes?”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Because scholars have caught some of the later additions by comparing manuscripts. But the earliest manuscripts are themselves 2nd-4th century copies, so changes made in the first century, before our evidence starts, can't be detected the same way.

In one breath

Mark 16:9-20 and John 7:53-8:11 are absent from the oldest manuscripts; 1 John 5:7 is a medieval addition; Matthew 28:19's formula clashes with Acts and with Eusebius' quotations; and scribes altered Luke 23:32, John 7:8 and Matthew 24:36 for theological reasons.

Argument 4 of 6

The Hebrew Bible shows deliberate theological changes

Thesis

Emanuel Tov, the leading textual critic of the Hebrew Bible, accepts that scribes made theological alterations, and the Dead Sea Scrolls preserve readings that the standard Hebrew text changed.

Argument
  1. P1If the standard Hebrew text (the Masoretic Text) contains deliberate changes, it is not the original revelation unchanged.
  2. P2Tov documents theological alterations accepted by most scholars, the Masoretic Text of Samuel is "frequently corrupt", and older editions of Jeremiah and Joshua survive in shorter forms.
  3. ∴The Hebrew Bible was edited and altered, which supports the Muslim position that the earlier scriptures were not preserved perfectly.
Evidence
  • Theological alterations. Tov writes that "the existence of theological changes… is accepted by most scholars".
  • Deuteronomy 32:8. The Masoretic Text says God divided the nations "according to the number of the sons of Israel". The Dead Sea Scroll 4QDeut-j and the Septuagint read "sons of God", which reflects the older idea of a heavenly council and was changed to avoid it.
  • Genesis 2:2. "On the seventh day God finished His work": the Samaritan Pentateuch, Septuagint and Peshitta read "on the sixth day", avoiding the implication that God worked on the Sabbath.
  • A lost paragraph. The Dead Sea Scroll 4QSam-a preserves a paragraph about Nahash the Ammonite before 1 Samuel 11 that dropped out of the Masoretic Text; some modern translations (NRSV) restore it.
  • Two editions. The Septuagint of Jeremiah is about one-eighth shorter and arranged differently; scholars recognise it as an earlier edition, with a Hebrew equivalent at Qumran (4QJer-b). Joshua also survives in a different edition.
  • The Masoretic Text of Samuel is "frequently corrupt", and translators often have to emend or bracket unintelligible readings.
  • Gospels quote the Greek. Mark 7:6-7 has Jesus quote Isaiah 29:13, "in vain do they worship me", which is the Septuagint wording, not the Hebrew.
Objections

"The Dead Sea Scrolls prove the text is accurate (Isaiah scroll)."

The Great Isaiah Scroll is close to the Masoretic Text, but the Scrolls also show multiple text forms circulating at once (proto-Masoretic, Septuagint-like, Samaritan-like), which is the opposite of a single preserved text.

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Deuteronomy 32:8 changes the theology of God's council, Genesis 2:2 changes what God did on the Sabbath, and Jeremiah exists in two editions of different length. Tov, a Jewish scholar, says most scholars accept that theological changes were made.

In one breath

Tov accepts deliberate theological changes in the Hebrew Bible: Deuteronomy 32:8 changed "sons of God" to "sons of Israel", Genesis 2:2 has competing days, a paragraph of Samuel was lost, and Jeremiah survives in two editions.

Thesis

Christians did not agree on which books belonged in the New Testament until the 4th century or later, and leading figures, including Reformers, rejected books that are now canonical.

Argument
  1. P1If God had preserved His revelation in the Bible, His followers would know which books are revealed.
  2. P2Revelation, Hebrews, James, Jude, 2 Peter and 2-3 John were disputed for centuries; some churches still use different canons; Luther and Zwingli rejected books.
  3. ∴The Bible's contents were decided by human judgement over time, not received as a fixed revelation.

  • Eusebius (c. 325) lists Revelation among both "accepted" and "rejected" books and lists James, Jude, 2 Peter and 2-3 John as "disputed" (Church History 3.25).
  • Revelation was omitted by Cyril of Jerusalem (Catechetical Lectures 4.36), Gregory of Nazianzus, the Synod of Laodicea, Chrysostom and Theodoret. "The church recognised, not decided, the canon."

Messianic "prophecies" refuted

7 arguments

Christian apologists often present a list of Old Testament prophecies that Jesus "fulfilled": the suffering servant of Isaiah 53, the pierced hands of Psalm 22, the virgin of Isaiah 7:14, the pierced one of Zechariah 12:10, the timetable of Daniel 9. This section takes each in context. The pattern is consistent: the passages are about Israel or a king of the prophet's own time, the key words are mistranslated, or the "prophecy" does not exist in the Old Testament at all. Muslims honour Jesus as the Messiah, so the point is not that Jesus was not the Messiah; it is that these texts do not prove the Christian claims about his death and divinity.

Argument 1 of 7

Isaiah 53: the suffering servant is Israel

Thesis

Isaiah repeatedly names the servant as Israel; in chapter 53 the speakers are the astonished nations; and the servant is promised offspring and long life, which does not fit a dying divine saviour.

Argument
  1. P1The identity of "the servant" must be determined from Isaiah's own usage and the context of chapters 52-53.
  2. P2Isaiah names the servant as Israel again and again; 52:15 introduces kings of the nations who are shocked; and the servant's reward is "offspring" and "prolonged days".
  3. ∴Isaiah 53 describes the suffering and vindication of Israel (or its righteous remnant), not the atoning death of God incarnate.
Evidence
  • The servant is named. "But you, Israel, My servant, Jacob whom I have chosen" (41:8); "Jacob My servant, Israel whom I have chosen" (44:1); "you are My servant, O Israel" (44:21; 49:3). The NT applies the servant passage of Isaiah 49:6 to Paul and Barnabas (Acts 13:47), so even the NT doesn't treat "servant" as a title for Jesus alone.
  • The speakers. 52:15 says "kings shall shut their mouths because of him; for what they had not been told they see". On this reading, chapter 53's "we" are the nations, astonished to see despised Israel exalted. The same theme appears in 49:7 ("kings shall see and arise… because of the LORD") and 60:14.
  • Plural. 53:8 says "for the transgression of my people, a stroke was to them (lamo)", a plural pronoun.
  • Offspring and long life. "He shall see his offspring (zera); he shall prolong his days" (53:10). Zera elsewhere in the Bible means physical descendants. A God-man who dies childless does not fit.
  • One ancient Jewish reading takes the passage as Israel; others, such as Targum Jonathan and b. Sanhedrin 98b, take it as the Messiah. Origen reports that a Jewish sage told him the passage refers "to the whole people regarded as one individual", in their dispersion and suffering (Contra Celsum 1.55). The Aramaic Targum makes the servant of 52:13 the Messiah, but transfers the suffering in chapter 53 to Israel in exile; the Messiah intercedes and rebuilds the Temple. He does not die.
  • Suffering for others is a national idea. Lamentations 5:7: "Our fathers sinned and are no more, and we bear their iniquities." 4 Maccabees (6:28-29; 17:21-22) says the martyrs' deaths atoned for Israel. None of this needs a divine victim.
  • "Cut off from the land of the living" (53:8) is used of Israel in exile: "our bones are dried up… we are cut off" (Ezek 37:11), before God brings them back to life.
Objections

"Israel is sinful, but the servant is innocent (53:9)."

The prophets distinguish the righteous remnant from the nation as a whole, and the passage describes Israel as the nations now see it: suffering unjustly at their hands. And the servant in 42:19 is "blind" and "deaf", which hardly fits a sinless Messiah either.

"Lamo can be singular in poetry."

It occasionally is, but its ordinary meaning is plural, and the plural fits the many explicit identifications of the servant as Israel.

"Jewish scholars only reinterpreted it as Israel after Christianity."

Origen, writing around 248, reports a Jewish interlocutor who read it of the whole people (Contra Celsum 1.55)

Your opponent says

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Read it with 52:13-15: the servant is "exalted", kings are shocked, and he sees "offspring" and "long days". The precise details Christians see (pierced, silent, buried with the rich) are general or mistranslated. Isaiah itself names the servant as Israel at least eight times.

In one breath

Argument 2 of 7

Thesis

The Hebrew of Psalm 22:16 says "like a lion [at] my hands and my feet", matching the animal imagery of the whole psalm. The Gospels quote Psalm 22 repeatedly but never verse 16.

Argument
  1. P1A prophecy proof needs the text actually to say what is claimed.
  2. P2The Masoretic Hebrew reads ka'ari, "like a lion"; the verb for "pierce" in Hebrew is different; and the Gospel writers, who quote Psalm 22 several times, never cite verse 16.

Paganism in Christianity and in Israelite religion

5 arguments

The Quran says the Christians' claims about Jesus "imitate the saying of those who disbelieved before them" (9:30). This section looks at what that means historically: the idea of a god "in the likeness of men", divine triads, a sacred meal of a god's flesh and blood, and image veneration were all features of the pagan world Christianity grew up in, and they entered Christian practice even though the Torah forbids them. It also shows that ancient Israelite religion itself absorbed pagan elements (a divine council, a goddess, the power of foreign gods), which the Quran's pure monotheism strips away. One caution: avoid the popular internet claims that Jesus was "copied" from Horus or Mithras in detail; those are exaggerated and easy to refute. The stronger argument is about concepts and practices, which is what this section uses.

Argument 1 of 5

  2. P2Greco-Roman religion was full of gods in human form and divine sons of gods; pagan crowds identified Paul and Barnabas as Zeus and Hermes; the language of Philippians 2:7 resembles Dionysus' self-description in Euripides.

Dilemmas for Christians

A dilemma forces the opponent to choose between two options, both of which cost him something. These are useful in debate because they move the burden of proof. Thesis

  2. P2Polygyny was practised by Abraham, Jacob, David and Solomon, regulated by the Law, allowed in the OT according to Tertullian (who forbade it to Christians), and not forbidden by Scripture according to Luther. The fathers required the veil. Canon law set marriage at twelve for girls.
  3. ∴Christian objections on these points are double standards.
Evidence
  • Polygyny. "If a man has two wives…" (Deut 21:15) regulates it. God says to David, "I gave you your master's wives into your arms" (2 Sam 12:8), and David "did what was right… except in the matter of Uriah" (1 Kgs 15:5). Tertullian says it was lawful for the patriarchs (To His Wife 1.2). Luther wrote in 1524 that he could not forbid a man to marry several wives because it does not contradict Scripture. Biblical scholar Dan McClellan: "the standard in the Bible is polygamy… nowhere identified as sinful." The Quran limits it to four with the condition of justice (4:3), and al-Shafi'i recommended limiting oneself to one.
  • The veil. Paul: "every wife who prays with her head uncovered dishonours her head… if a wife will not cover her head, let her cut her hair short" (1 Cor 11:5-6). Clement of Alexandria: a woman should go out "with her head veiled and her face covered" (Paedagogus 3.11). The Apostolic Tradition (18.5) requires women to cover their heads with an opaque cloth. Tertullian wrote a whole treatise On the Veiling of Virgins.
  • Age of marriage. The Protevangelium of James (2nd century) says Mary was entrusted to an elderly Joseph at twelve. Medieval canon law set the minimum age of marriage at twelve for girls and fourteen for boys, with betrothal from seven. Augustine's fiancée was about two years under the age of marriage when he was in his early thirties (Confessions 6.13).
  • Women's testimony. In Luke 24:11 the apostles dismiss the women's report as "an idle tale". Josephus says that women's testimony was not admitted "on account of the levity and boldness of their sex" (Antiquities 4.219). Jesus told his followers to obey the Pharisees' teaching (Matt 23:2-3).
  • The fathers on women. Tertullian called woman "the devil's gateway" (On the Apparel of Women 1.1).
Objections

"The New Testament moved beyond polygamy."

No NT verse explicitly forbids it for ordinary believers; 1 Timothy 3:2 limits overseers to one wife, which implies others had more. And Luther, reading the same NT, could not forbid it.

In one breath

Abraham, Jacob, David and Solomon had several wives, and Luther couldn't forbid it; Paul and Clement required the veil; canon law allowed girls to marry at twelve. Critics who attack Islam on these points condemn their own tradition.

The Church's own moral record

Thesis

Indulgences sold for money, heretics executed by Church teaching, unbaptised infants consigned to hell, the Crusades and the Inquisition: the Church's moral record does not entitle critics to treat Christianity as morally superior.

Argument
  1. P1If Christians judge Islam by the conduct of Muslims, the same test applies to Christianity.
  2. P2The Church sold remission of punishment for money, taught that heretics deserve death, taught that unbaptised infants are damned (Augustine) and left to a limbo theory, and launched wars that killed millions, including Christians and Jews.
  3. ∴Judged by the conduct of its institutions, Christianity has no moral advantage.
Evidence
  • Indulgences. Pope Leo X authorised indulgences to fund St Peter's Basilica; the preacher Johann Tetzel was associated with the slogan "as soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs". Luther's 95 Theses (1517) attacked this. The Catholic Encyclopedia admits the abuses, which continued until the Council of Trent.
  • Infants in hell. Augustine taught that unbaptised infants are damned and that promising them heaven is heresy (On the Soul and its Origin 1.9-11).
  • Heretics. Thomas Aquinas taught that heretics, after a second warning, may be handed to the secular power "to be exterminated from the world by death" (Summa II-II, q. 11, a. 3). Emperor Theodosius' edict of 380 made Nicene Christianity compulsory.

  • Prostitution. Augustine wrote, "remove prostitutes from society and you will disturb everything with lust" (On Order 2.4.12), and some medieval bishops (e.g. the Bishop of Winchester) drew income from brothels.
Objections

"These are people's sins, not Christianity's teaching."

Indulgence sales were papal policy, and infant damnation and the death of heretics were taught by Augustine and Aquinas, though Aquinas' view was also condemned in effect by Exsurge Domine (1520)

In one breath

Popes sold indulgences, Augustine damned unbaptised infants, Aquinas taught heretics should be killed, and the Crusades massacred Jews and sacked Constantinople. The Church is in no position to judge Islam by its followers' conduct.

Christian miracle claims and later traditions

2 arguments

Christians sometimes argue that modern miracles (apparitions of Mary, Eucharistic wonders, healings) prove their faith. This section explains why such events cannot certify a religion, then looks at practices like Christmas and the papacy that have no basis in the New Testament.

Argument 1 of 2

What a miracle can and cannot prove

Thesis

A miracle is evidence for a prophet only when it accompanies a claim to prophethood and a message. Apparitions and wonders without a claimant or message prove nothing, every religion reports them, and the Bible itself warns that deceptive signs occur.

Argument
  1. P1A sign authenticates a message only if someone claims to bring the message and points to the sign as proof (as Moses did before Pharaoh).
  2. P2Modern Christian wonders (Zeitoun 1968, Fatima 1917, bleeding hosts) have no prophet claiming a new revelation (Fatima had messages, but none that claimed to supersede the Gospel); similar wonders are reported in Hinduism and other religions; and the Bible says Satan "disguises himself as an angel of light" (2 Cor 11:14) and that false prophets show signs (Deut 13:1-3; Matt 24:24).
  3. ∴Such wonders cannot establish Christianity over Islam or any other religion.
Evidence
  • The criterion. In the Quran and the Bible, signs come with prophets as proof of their message (Exod 4:1-9; Quran 7:106-108). An unexplained light or image with no message is not a credential for anyone.
  • Apparitions. Witness accounts of the Fatima "miracle of the sun" (1917) contradict each other; Kevin McClure's study documents the inconsistencies. The Zeitoun lights (1968) carried no message.
  • Other religions. In 1995 statues of Ganesha across India were reported to "drink milk"; scientists explained it by capillary action. If such events prove a religion, they prove Hinduism too.
  • Eucharistic miracles. Catholic sites report consecrated hosts bleeding "type AB blood". Kelly Kearse, a Catholic immunologist, has written that bacteria can share surface antigens with blood types, so AB typing does not show a human source.
  • Relics. In the Middle Ages several churches claimed to hold Jesus' foreskin (the "Holy Prepuce"); Marc Shell documents the competition. A relic economy is not evidence of truth.
  • The Bible's warning. "False christs and false prophets will arise and perform great signs and wonders, so as to lead astray, if possible, even the elect" (Matt 24:24).
Objections

"Muslims also report miracles of saints."

Islam does not base its truth on them. The Quran rests its case on the Quran itself, the Prophet's ﷺ fulfilled prophecies and his character, which are public and checkable. Karamat of saints are not proof of Islam.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Millions saw the sun dance at Fatima. How do you explain that?”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

The accounts conflict, people at other locations saw nothing, and staring at the sun produces visual effects. But even granting something happened, it came with no prophet and no new revelation. The Bible itself says deceptive signs will appear (Matt 24:24; 2 Cor 11:14).

In one breath

Miracles authenticate a prophet's message; Fatima, Zeitoun and bleeding hosts have no prophet, other religions report equivalent wonders, and the Bible warns of deceptive signs. They prove nothing.

Argument 2 of 2

Christmas and the papacy have no New Testament basis

Thesis

Two of the most visible features of Christianity, the feast of Christmas and the authority of the Pope, are absent from the NT and the earliest church.

Argument
  1. P1If a practice or office was instituted by Jesus or the apostles, it should appear in the NT or the earliest Christian writings.
  2. P2No NT text mentions celebrating Jesus' birth, and the earliest bishops (Clement of Rome, Ignatius) know nothing of a universal Roman authority.
  3. ∴Both developed later and are traditions, not apostolic revelation.
Evidence
  • Christmas. The NT never mentions a feast of Jesus' birth or its date. The sermons in Acts focus on the resurrection; Paul never mentions a birth festival. The 25 December date is first attested in the 4th century.
  • The papacy. 1 Clement (c. 96), written from Rome to Corinth, speaks for "the church of God in Rome", not for a single bishop with universal jurisdiction. Ignatius of Antioch writes to six churches emphasising their bishop, but his letter to Rome mentions no bishop at all and describes the Roman church as the one that "presides in the region of the Romans".
Objections

"Matthew 16:18 makes Peter the rock and founds the papacy."

It mentions Peter, not Rome or successors. Eastern Orthodox and Protestant churches read it differently, and the earliest sources show no universal Roman jurisdiction.

Practice

Your opponent says

“Muslims celebrate the Prophet's birthday too.”

Answer it in your head first, then compare.

Many Muslim scholars reject the Mawlid as an innovation precisely because it has no basis in the Quran or Sunnah. Islam distinguishes revelation from later custom; the point is that Christianity has no such check.

In one breath

The NT never mentions Christmas, and 25 December appears in the 4th century. Clement of Rome writes for a church, not a pope, and Ignatius' letter to Rome mentions no bishop.
